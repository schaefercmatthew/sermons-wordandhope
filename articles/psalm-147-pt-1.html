The God Who Counts Every Star Also Heals Every Broken Heart

Psalm 147 puts two ideas next to each other that most of us keep in separate rooms: a God big enough to name a hundred billion stars, and a God who bends down to bind up your wounds. It turns out one is the reason you can trust the other.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

You have probably run into a version of this problem before, even if you never put words to it. The more impressive something is, the more distant it tends to feel. A company big enough to matter rarely has a CEO who calls you back personally. Size and attention trade against each other almost everywhere you look, so most people quietly assume the same trade governs God: the bigger he is, the less he could possibly be thinking about you.

Psalm 147 was written to break exactly that assumption. It puts the size of God and the tenderness of God in the same breath, on purpose, and it wants you to feel the weight of both at once. Strength is not the opposite of mercy here. It is the reason mercy can be trusted at all.

Written for People Coming Home from Nothing

This psalm was almost certainly written after the exile. A full generation of Israelite families had been scattered across Babylon, their city burned, their temple gone. Now they were coming home, walls rebuilt, families reunited. But look closely at how the psalm describes it. It does not credit the people for finally rebuilding their own walls, and it does not credit King Cyrus, who sent them home with the money and permission to do it. It credits the Lord. He builds. He gathers. Nobody in Jerusalem rebuilt their own walls by sheer determination, and nobody scattered to the winds gathered themselves back. That framing matters before you read another line, because the whole psalm keeps making the same point at bigger and smaller scales: this is a God who does the work his people cannot do for themselves.

The Text: Praise Built on Power and Tenderness

"Praise the Lord! For it is good to sing praises to our God, for it is pleasant, and a song of praise is fitting. The Lord builds up Jerusalem; he gathers the outcasts of Israel. He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds. He determines the number of the stars; he gives to all of them their names. Great is our Lord, and abundant in power; his understanding is beyond measure. The Lord lifts up the humble; he casts the wicked to the ground. Sing to the Lord with thanksgiving; make melody to our God on the lyre! He covers the heavens with clouds; he prepares rain for the earth; he makes grass grow on the hills. He gives to the beasts their food, and to the young ravens that cry. His delight is not in the strength of the horse, nor his pleasure in the legs of a man, but the Lord takes pleasure in those who fear him, in those who hope in his steadfast love."

Psalm 147:1–11

Verse one gives the command to praise, and then three reasons for it. The psalmist is not saying you owe God praise the way you owe a debt or a tax. He is saying it is good, pleasant, and fitting. Praise is not a chore bolted onto the Christian life from the outside. It is the thing your soul was built for, the way swimming belongs to a fish or singing belongs to a bird. Matthew Henry put it well: praising God is work for its own wages. It is heaven brought down to earth.

Think about how natural it feels to say thank you when someone hands you a gift you actually needed. You do not have to work your way up to that; it is the obvious response. That is what this psalm means by fitting: not a religious performance, but the right, enjoyable thing to do once you see who God actually is.

A Builder, a Physician, and a Namer of Stars

Verse two starts with God as builder and gatherer. First Peter picks up the same idea about the church: "You yourselves, like living stones, are being built up into a spiritual house" (1 Peter 2:5). It does not say you are building yourselves into that house. You are being built. The church is not a group of people who managed to assemble themselves into something respectable. God is the builder, in Jerusalem and in his people now.

Verse three moves from builder to physician: "He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds." This might be the one line from this psalm you already had memorized without knowing where it came from. Picture what is actually happening: God gathering his people the way a parent gathers scattered children, then getting down to bind up the wound himself, like a doctor who does not mind getting his hands dirty. Spurgeon said of this verse that God never lost a case yet. There was never one who came to him with a broken heart that he did not heal. Jesus says something with the same shape in Matthew 11: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest."

Then, right after that tender picture, verse four turns and says God determines the number of the stars and gives to all of them their names. That is not a small pivot. We now know from modern astronomy that our galaxy alone holds something in the neighborhood of a hundred billion stars, and there are roughly a hundred billion galaxies beyond our own. Those are still just estimates, because that is what we do. We estimate. God does not estimate. He knows every one of those hundred billion times a hundred billion stars. He does not fling them into space and let gravity sort out where they land. He names them, the way a shepherd knows his own sheep individually rather than counting heads from a distance.

His scale is not a barrier to his attention. It is the reason you can trust his affection, because of how big he is.

The psalmist did not set the healing of broken hearts next to the counting of galaxies by accident. He is building an argument. You might expect a God who tracks every star in every galaxy to be too big to notice you, to have so much to manage that he loses you in the crowd. This psalm says the opposite. Verse five backs it up: "Great is our Lord, and abundant in power; his understanding is beyond measure." A lot of us get our theology backward here, assuming that the bigger God gets, the more distant and abstract he feels, or that the closer he feels, the smaller and more manageable he must actually be. Psalm 147 will not let you split those two truths apart. He heals broken hearts, and you can trust that because of his abundant power, not in spite of it.

What This Means

The same abundant power that names a hundred billion stars is what makes this God able to actually keep the promise to bind up your specific wound. A smaller God could not guarantee that. This one can.

The God Who Runs the Galaxy Also Notices One Hungry Bird

Verse six turns from God's greatness to what he does with it: "The Lord lifts up the humble; he casts the wicked to the ground." This God who counts galaxies has an opinion about the posture of your heart. He is not too occupied with the machinery of the cosmos to notice whether you are leaning on him or relying on yourself. Some readers have called this the hinge of the psalm, because everything before it is about God's greatness, and everything after it is about the posture God favors: humility and hope, not strength and self-sufficiency.

Verses seven through nine zoom the camera in. From the highest heavens and the stars, the psalm narrows to weather patterns, then to rain, then to grass on a particular hillside, then to specific creatures who need food, and finally all the way down to one young raven with its mouth open, crying out. Theologians have a word for God's ongoing care for everything he has made: providence. Verses four and five describe what is called general providence, God's care for the whole created order at once, every galaxy held together, every season on schedule. Verses seven through nine describe something narrower, called special providence: God's particular attention to particular creatures, this raven, on this hillside, in this rain shower.

The raven is not a random choice. Under the law given to Moses, the raven is a ceremonially unclean bird, listed in both Deuteronomy 14 and Leviticus. It has no religious standing, nothing to recommend it. It is not an eagle. Of every creature the psalmist could have picked to make his point, he picked the one with the least going for it. Job asks almost the same question about this exact bird: "Who provides for the raven its food, when its young ones cry to God?" (Job 38:41). Jesus takes up the same argument in Matthew 6, telling his listeners to consider the birds of the air, who neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet are fed by their heavenly Father.

General providence tells you God is great enough to run everything. Special providence tells you he is near enough to run your life. And if his special providence reaches all the way down to a bird with no covenant and nothing to give, that tells you something about how far it reaches for you, a person he did not spare his own Son for.

What God Is Actually Looking For

Verses ten and eleven bring the whole argument to a point: "His delight is not in the strength of the horse, nor his pleasure in the legs of a man, but the Lord takes pleasure in those who fear him, in those who hope in his steadfast love." In the ancient world, a horse was a war machine. A nation with horses and chariots had security. A nation without them was at the mercy of nations that had them. Horses functioned as something like a bank account and an insurance policy rolled into one, the thing you pointed to when someone asked how you would survive the next threat.

Scripture keeps circling back to this image on purpose. Deuteronomy warns kings not to acquire many horses or send the people back to Egypt to get them (Deuteronomy 17:16), because Egypt is where the horses were, and Egypt is also where the chains were. Every time Israel went looking for horses, she was walking back toward the house of slavery God had already carried her out of. Psalm 20:7 says it plainly: some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the Lord our God.

"Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses, who trust in chariots because they are many and in horsemen because they are very strong, but do not look to the Holy One of Israel or consult the Lord!"

Isaiah 31:1

That is Isaiah's word for a generation that went looking for security in exactly this way. Horses do not move God. He has no shortage that your version of a horse could cover. What he asks for instead is simple to say and hard to produce on your own: fear him, and hope in his steadfast love. Left to yourself, you do not reach for God first. You reach for the horse first, every time.

What Is Your Horse?

We do not usually reach for literal horses now. Maybe it is a number in your bank account, a certain amount of health, a reputation as the person who fixes what is broken, or a plan mapped out so nothing catches you exposed. Whatever it is, you reach for it before you reach for the God who made the stars, and verse six already told you why. It is pride, and pride cannot talk itself out of pride. Something has to happen from the outside.

Something did. On a Friday afternoon outside Jerusalem, the casting-down of verse six happened to the one man who never once reached for a horse, who trusted God perfectly all the way to Gethsemane and did not flinch away from it. He was cast down in the place of everyone who has spent a lifetime reaching for horses instead of him, so that you could be lifted up.

Fearing God and hoping in his love is not something you manufacture by sheer effort. God's love in this psalm was not proven by how well Israel trusted him. It was proven at a cross and an empty tomb, where God kept his covenant love to sinners at the highest possible cost to himself. If you are united to Christ by faith, his fear of the Father, perfect where yours has always been divided, is credited to you. You are not standing right with God because you finally gripped your fear and hope tightly enough. You are standing right with him because his Son gripped them perfectly and handed you the credit.

What This Asks of You

Name your horse specifically. Not "I struggle with anxiety" in the abstract, but the actual thing you reach for before you reach for God: the number in an account, the plan that leaves nothing exposed, the reputation you cannot afford to lose. Naming it honestly is the first step, because it was never going to be what saved you anyway.

Why This Changes How You Read Your Own Week

The God who is great enough to number a hundred billion stars is the God who is great enough to heal the brokenhearted, and he did not stop at tender feelings toward you. He sent his Son to be cast down into the dust so that everyone with no strength left, nothing to bring but a broken heart, could be lifted up in his place. You did not run that. Someone else already ran it for you, all the way to the cross and into the grave, and what he won there is simply being handed to you now.

So when you read verse one again, the command makes more sense than it did the first time through. Praise the Lord, because it is good, an obvious response to news this large. Praise him because it is pleasant, an enjoyable truth to sit with rather than a duty to survive. Praise him because in Christ, you are exactly the kind of person this God delights in. Nothing would be more fitting.

If this week has you reaching for a horse again, you do not have to pretend otherwise. Name it, and bring it to the one who counts stars and binds up wounds. He has never lost a case yet.
