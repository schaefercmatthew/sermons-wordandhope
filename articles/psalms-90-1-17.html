Teach Us to Number Our Days: Moses's Prayer in Psalm 90

Moses prays from the gap between God's eternity and our few, hard years — asking not for information about death, but for a different heart altogether.

Matthew Schaefer · July 19, 2026

A Home Before There Was a Home

Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations. Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever you had formed the earth and the world, from everlasting to everlasting, you are God. Psalm 90:1-2

Sit with that word for a minute: dwelling place. Not a place you visit, not a shelter for a season, but a place you live, a place you belong, even when nothing else is stable. Moses writes it in the past tense on purpose — not "you will be" our dwelling place, and not merely "you are," but "you have been," across every generation before his own and every one after. It included his own wandering, homeless-feeling people in the wilderness. It includes you, wherever you're reading this today.

We're wired to need a home — not just a roof, but a place we belong, where we're welcomed no matter how far we've wandered. Jesus tells a story about a son who leaves chasing freedom, and when the money runs out and he's feeding pigs, Luke tells us he doesn't picture some far country anymore. He pictures home. Moses says that's God himself. Not a house, not a tent, not even the promised land — God.

Before You Feel the Weight, Feel the Foundation

Moses doesn't lean on God's goodness or the trustworthiness of his promises to make this case. Instead, he leans on God's eternal nature. Before the mountains, about as old and solid as anything we can point to, God already was. Before the earth and the world existed at all, he already was. From everlasting to everlasting — a phrase our minds can't really hold, since everything we know had a starting point. God didn't.

This matters because Moses is about to draw a stark contrast between God's permanence and our frailty, and he wants us to feel the weight of verses 1 and 2 before he says a single word about how short our lives are. As Patrick Miller put it, "Finding meaning in the face of death depends on placing the starting point of our understanding outside ourselves." We have to see how permanent God is before we can rightly see how temporary we are.

Dust, and Less Than a Watch in the Night

Verse 3 turns the question to us: "You return man to dust, and say, Return, O children of man." The Hebrew word for man is Adam — dust man, ground man. Genesis 2 tells us how he got that name; Genesis 3 tells us where he's headed. And this isn't about one person's death or a hard case — "children of man" means every son, every daughter, all of us, made of the same dust, headed to the same ground.

Try to picture a thousand years — not a hundred, a thousand. Further back than roughly thirty-five generations of grandparents. Before this nation existed. Moses says to God, that's like yesterday — not even a full day gone by. In fact, it's like a watch in the night, three or four hours a soldier stands half-awake and barely remembers by morning. And we aren't even a thousand years. We're seventy, maybe eighty. Verses 5-6 pile on three images without pausing to explain: swept away like a flood, gone before you can hold your ground; like a dream, vivid while you're in it and gone ten minutes after waking; like grass, flourishing in the morning and withered by evening. Isaiah 40 and 1 Peter 1 reach for the same image, and both times, the point isn't just that grass withers, but that the word of the Lord remains forever. Something fading, set right next to something that never does.

Why We Die

Moses doesn't leave the question hanging. Verse 7: "We are brought to an end by your anger. By your wrath we are dismayed." Death isn't nature simply running its course. Romans 5:12 says sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin. Romans 6:23 calls death the wages of sin. Every grave is a monument to something having gone catastrophically wrong.

Verse 8 goes further: "our iniquities... our secret sins in the light of your presence." Not just public failures — the sin hidden so well you've almost convinced yourself it isn't there, hidden even from yourself. C.S. Lewis said it plainly: "In the end, that face which is the delight or the terror of the universe must be turned upon each of us." All our days pass away under his wrath; we bring our years to an end "like a sigh" — not a triumphant conclusion, not even a peaceful fade. Just a breath.

If you belong to Christ, this needs care. Death is still the enemy — 1 Corinthians 15:26 calls it the last thing to be destroyed, and Revelation pictures it thrown into the lake of fire. It was never part of the original design, and it feels wrong because it is wrong. But Romans 8 says there's no condemnation for those in Christ — the wrath Moses describes here, Christ has already taken on himself. What remains for the believer isn't punishment; it's simply living in a world still under the curse, while the debt itself is paid.

The Best Case Scenario Is Still Toil

The years of our life are seventy, or even by reason of strength eighty; yet their span is but toil and trouble; they are soon gone, and we fly away. Psalm 90:10

Notice Moses isn't describing tragedy here — no early accident, no diagnosis no one saw coming. This is the best case: a long life. And he calls even that "toil and trouble." We feel this. Strapped financially, working to keep up as everything costs more. Fighting for marriages and friendships. Fighting for purity against a culture working against us. Fighting to teach our children right from wrong. And for what? Centuries later, Solomon would sit down and write an entire book turning this same observation over from every angle — Ecclesiastes reads like an extended meditation on this one verse.

Verse 11 asks, almost rhetorically, "Who considers the power of your anger... according to the fear of you?" The honest answer: nobody, naturally. We're too caught up in the daily fight to gain that perspective on our own — like asking an ant, mid-scurry, to stop and solve a math problem. It's not refusal. It's incapacity.

Key Point

Notice Moses isn't describing tragedy here — no early accident, no diagnosis no one saw coming.

The Hinge: Teach Us

Nobody teaches themselves their own blind spots.

Everything up to this point has been building toward verse 12: "So teach us to number our days, that we may get a heart of wisdom." Moses doesn't ask God to help him remember something he basically knows. He asks to be taught — the word for something you cannot get on your own, requiring a teacher outside yourself. He already told us why: nobody naturally considers God's wrath. We're not withholding wisdom out of stubbornness; we're incapable of producing it ourselves.

"Number our days" doesn't mean know abstractly that life is short; we already know that. It means actually count them, feel the smallness of the number, live like someone who knows he has a limited number of mornings left rather than someone vaguely aware of it in theory. Christians across history took this seriously enough to build habits around it — paintings of Jerome always show a human skull on his desk, a daily physical reminder that life is short. Al Mohler has written that he keeps one too, as a reminder that every day moves one day from "future" to "past." The Puritan prayer book The Valley of Vision includes a bedtime prayer: "May my frequent lying down make me familiar with death. The bed I approach remind me of the grave... may I hold life loosely in my hands, knowing that I receive it on condition of its surrender."

And the goal isn't a head full of facts about mortality. It's a heart of wisdom, because the heart is where the actual problem lives. Jeremiah 17:9 says the heart is deceitful above all else; Proverbs 4:23 tells us to guard it above everything, "for from it flow the springs of life." The secret sins of verse 8 didn't start as actions; they started as a heart wanting something other than God. So Moses isn't asking for better information. He's asking God to change what the heart actually wants.

A Bolder Prayer Than It Sounds

"Return, O Lord, how long? Have pity on your servants" (v.13). This word "return" appeared once already, back in verse 3, where it meant judgment — God returning man to dust. Moses uses the same Hebrew word Exodus 32 records him praying after the golden calf, when God's anger burned hot and Moses pleaded for God to turn back his wrath. And God did. Moses isn't throwing words at the sky hoping something sticks. He's a man who has stood in the gap before and watched God relent — which tells us we can pray with that same boldness, not as people unsure anyone is listening, but as people who know this God can be pleaded with.

He prays for gladness to match affliction, "for as many days as you have afflicted us, for as many years as we have seen evil" (v.15) — day for day, year for year. Already a bold ask. But we get to pray this knowing more than Moses could see. Paul, in the middle of his own suffering, says "our light and momentary affliction is preparing us for an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison" (2 Corinthians 4:17) — not a matched trade, but an absurdly lopsided one in our favor, because Christ took the wrath at the cross so that all that's left for us is glory.

Establish the Work of Our Hands

Moses's prayer doesn't end with himself. Verse 16 asks that God's work be shown "to their children," reaching past the people in front of him into a generation not yet born. Then the psalm lands: "establish the work of our hands... yes, establish the work of our hands" (v.17), said once and immediately repeated. He isn't asking to erase the toil named back in verse 10. He's asking God to make that labor count — to take work that would otherwise vanish with the person who did it, and give it a weight that lasts.

Here's what makes that prayer answerable, though Moses could only see a shadow of it: Christ's work on the cross is finished, established forever, and nothing can undo it. Because his work is secure, our small, short work gets grafted onto something permanent. Paul tells the Corinthians to be steadfast, "knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58). Spurgeon put it well: "we come and go, but the Lord's work abides... since it is far more his work than ours, he will secure it immortally. When we've withered like grass, our holy service... will survive the fire."

There's real sadness in this psalm's middle section — but sadness isn't given the first word or the last. It opens with God as our dwelling place and closes with a prayer for gladness and lasting work. Our frailty is real; we need to feel it. But it isn't the whole story, and it doesn't get the final word. God does.
