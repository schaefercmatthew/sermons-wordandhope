You're Not a Tenant of God. You're His Child.

Psalm 147 spends its first half showing you a God who feeds every raven that cries. Its second half asks a sharper question: does he only provide for you, or does he claim you? The answer changes everything.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

A landlord keeps the building standing because a building falling down is bad for business. That is a real kindness, and tenants are right to expect it. But that same man goes home at night and feeds his own children, and he does not do it to protect an investment. He does it because they are his. Those are two different things wearing the same clothes, and most of us have quietly assumed God only does the first one for us.

Psalm 147:12-20 says otherwise. The first half of this psalm, verses 1-11, showed a God who provides, one who names every star and still stoops to feed a young raven nobody else is watching. This second half shows something with more weight to it: a God who claims a people as his own, the way a father claims a son, not the way a landlord manages a tenant. And by the end of it, that claim reaches past the nation it was written about and lands on you.

What the First Half Already Told You

The first eleven verses of this psalm answered a question you may not have asked out loud: if God is great enough to number every star in the sky, does he still notice you? The psalm's answer was providence, God's ongoing care for everything that exists. General providence is the wide-angle picture, every galaxy held in place, every season arriving on schedule. Special providence is the same care with the camera pulled in tight: clouds, then rain, then grass on one particular hillside, then a young raven crying because no one has fed it yet. The raven has no covenant with God and nothing to offer him. He feeds it anyway, because that is who he characteristically is. Jesus makes the same argument in Matthew 6: look at the birds of the air, and consider how much more your Father will do for you.

That argument runs from lesser to greater, and it left off on purpose at a question: if God's care reaches all the way down to an unclean bird, how much further does it reach for people he actually calls his own? Verses 12-20 are where the psalm finally answers that, and the answer is not simply "more of the same care." It is something different in kind.

The Text: From Provision to Claim

Here is the second half of the psalm, in full:

"Praise the Lord, O Jerusalem! Praise your God, O Zion! For he strengthens the bars of your gates; he blesses your children within you. He makes peace in your borders; he fills you with the finest of wheat. He sends out his command to the earth; his word runs swiftly. He gives snow like wool; he scatters frost like ashes. He hurls down his crystals of ice like crumbs; who can stand before his cold? He sends out his word, and melts them; he makes his wind blow, and the waters flow. He declares his word to Jacob, his statutes and rules to Israel. He has not dealt thus with any other nation; they do not know his rules. Praise the Lord!"

Psalm 147:12–20

Reading the Second Half Point by Point

A command that gets more specific

Verse 12 opens with the same word that opened verse 1: praise the Lord. But look at who is being told to praise him. Verse 1 is a command for everyone, and by the time you read through the surrounding psalms, it is a command for everything in the created order. Verse 12 narrows that command down to one people: Jerusalem, Zion. Even the wording tightens. "Praise God" would already be true of God's ownership over all things. The psalmist writes "praise your God" instead, and that claims something in the other direction. God owns Jerusalem, yes, but Jerusalem, in some real sense, gets to call him hers.

A landlord's upkeep and a father's provision

Verses 13 and 14 stack up four specific gifts: secure gates, blessed children, peace in the borders, and wheat, described in the original language as "the fatness of the wheat." Every nation gets some measure of God's kindness. It rains on the just and the unjust. That is common grace, and it is real. But not every nation gets its gates strengthened and its children blessed on purpose. This is not the general upkeep a landlord gives a building because a building falling down is bad for business. This is what a father does for children who are his, because they are his.

A landlord keeps the building standing. A father feeds his kids because they're his kids, not because it's on a schedule.

I used to work at a residential treatment center in Texas, caring for teenage boys who had aged out of nearly every other chance the foster system gives a kid. Some nights I'd be dead tired by eleven o'clock, breaking up fights all day, and I still had to go check on every one of them, on schedule, medication logged, whether they'd behaved that week or not. It wasn't indifference. It was real care. But it isn't how it works with my own daughter. If I check on her at eleven o'clock, it isn't because I have a care log to fill out. It's because she's my daughter. That is the distance between verses 13-14 and everything that came before them in this psalm. It is the distance between a job and a claim.

Snow that obeys, and a people who are addressed

Verses 15-18 pull the camera back out to general providence: God's word goes out, and snow falls like wool, frost scatters like ashes, ice comes down like crumbs, and then his word goes out again and melts all of it. This isn't a step backward from the personal claim in verses 13-14. It's building toward a contrast. Nothing in that list of snow and frost and wind negotiates with God. It doesn't deliberate. It has no opinion about whether it agrees. It simply obeys, immediately and completely, because that is the only kind of relationship weather is capable of having with the one who made it.

Then verse 19 turns, and the language changes shape: "He declares his word to Jacob, his statutes and rules to Israel." Snow receives a command. Israel receives an address. Those are different kinds of speech from the same mouth, and the distance between them is the distance between God's care for everything he made and the particular claim he holds on his own people.

A claim that was never earned

Verse 20 makes the claim explicit: "He has not dealt thus with any other nation; they do not know his rules." Moses says nearly the same thing in Deuteronomy 4: no other people has ever heard the voice of God out of the fire and lived, and no other god has ever gone and taken a nation for himself out of the middle of another nation, the way the Lord did in Egypt. God repeats it again in Amos 3: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth." This was never a reward for something Israel brought to the table. Deuteronomy 7 says plainly that the Lord did not choose Israel because they were more numerous than other nations. They were the fewest of all peoples. He chose them because he loved them, and that is the entire explanation Scripture gives. Not earned. Chosen.

The Wall This Verse Draws, and the Wall Christ Tore Down

Read plainly, on its own, verse 20 draws a wall and leaves most of us standing outside it. "He has not dealt thus with any other nation" was written about Israel, not about a room full of Gentiles in a small town in America two thousand years later. That should bother you if you're not ethnically Israel, because on its face, this claim was never made about you.

Paul deals with exactly this wall in Ephesians 2. Writing to Gentile believers, he says: "Remember that you were at that time separated from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers to the covenant of promise, having no hope and without God in the world" (Ephesians 2:12). That is Paul applying Psalm 147:20 honestly to people who were never Israel, never Judah, never inside the wall the psalm describes. But he doesn't stop there. Verse 13 continues: "Now in Christ Jesus you who were once far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ." And verse 19: "So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God."

First Peter 2 says the same thing in language borrowed directly from Israel's own story. Quoting the Old Testament, Peter writes that whoever believes in the cornerstone God laid in Zion will not be put to shame, and then, astonishingly, applies Israel's identity straight onto the church: "You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession... Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people" (1 Peter 2:9-10). That language was Israel's alone. Peter hands it to you.

What This Means

The wall Psalm 147:20 draws around Israel is the same wall Christ tore down at the cross. What belonged to Jacob alone now belongs, in Christ, to anyone united to him by faith.

Provided For, or Adopted?

Galatians 4 tells you how that wall actually came down, and it wasn't by announcement. "When the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons" (Galatians 4:4-5). Jesus lived under the law Israel was given and died under the curse of breaking it, in your place, so that the same blood that brought you near in Ephesians 2 is the blood that purchased your adoption here. You are not a slave anymore. You're a son, or a daughter, and if a child, then an heir.

So ask yourself honestly which category you've been living in. Do you relate to God like a tenant who is grateful the building is kept standing, thankful for a paycheck's worth of blessing and nothing more? Or do you actually believe he has claimed you, the way a father claims a child, not because you earned a place on the lease but because he loved you and adopted you at a cost to himself? Those two postures produce completely different lives. A tenant negotiates. A tenant keeps a bag half-packed. A child rests.

What This Asks of You Now

Think of Chris. I knew a boy by that name at the treatment center I mentioned, seventeen years old, one of the kinder kids in the whole place, smart, hardworking, and completely without a family. Nobody wanted an older boy with his record. Most of the boys there had given up hoping for adoption and were just counting down the days until they turned eighteen and the system let them go. Chris wanted more than that. He wanted a family badly enough to finish high school for it, and by the time I left, he had been given a chance with a foster family. I never found out if it became permanent. But I remember the whole camp celebrating that day, counselors included, because a chance like that was rare there.

God did not deal with you the way Chris was dealt with. Chris got a chance. You got adopted, by the grace of the Father and the blood of the Son, and no court can revisit that verdict.

Where This Lands

If you belong to Christ, stop relating to God like a landlord you're hoping won't raise the rent. He strengthens your gates, blesses your children, and fills your table, not because it's good business, but because you're his.

Look at where this psalm starts and where it ends: praise the Lord, verse 1, praise the Lord, verse 20. That's the only response this much love from God actually leaves room for. You stop managing the relationship carefully from a distance, the way you'd manage a landlord, and you praise him instead, easily, once you actually believe you were never a tenant here.
