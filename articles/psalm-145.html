Two Ways to Pray a Psalm Back to God

If prayer is learning to speak with God, we should begin the same way Jesus began: with adoration, not requests. Psalm 145 and two simple ways to pray Scripture back to God.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

Watch a child learn to talk. First they hear a word, over and over, from a parent. Then they try to say it. They get it wrong. They hear it again. They try again. Slowly, by listening and practicing and listening again, a five-year-old ends up sounding remarkably like their own parent: the same words, the same phrases, sometimes the very same inflection. It's the only way anyone has ever learned to speak.

That's exactly how Christians learn to pray. We hear our Father's words in Scripture, we practice praying them back, and over time His words start showing up in our own. This begins a new series on learning to pray the Psalms, not verse-by-verse exposition this time, but something more practical: how to actually use these prayers God has already given us.

A Society That Has Lost Its Solitude

Secular studies on isolation turn up something strange: social interaction is sharply down, yet people report almost never being truly alone. The moment solitude arrives, a phone comes out, a podcast starts, a screen fills the silence. Watch people in a checkout line, or at a restaurant the instant conversation lulls. We've traded solitude for constant noise, and where solitude disappears, prayer disappears with it. We live, on top of that, in a culture that praises busyness. Ask how someone's doing and you'll rarely hear "I've had a lot of time alone lately"; you'll hear "things are crazy right now."

When many were asked directly why they skip prayer meeting, the honest answer wasn't busyness. It was: "I don't know how to pray." That's not a personal failure. It's the same thing the disciples said to Jesus in Luke 11: "Lord, teach us to pray." They didn't know either. Many never grew up hearing prayer modeled regularly, beyond a quick blessing before a meal. Where would anyone have learned? That's exactly the gap this series is meant to close, using the one book of Scripture that covers nearly every facet of human experience in prayer form: anger, grief, joy, vengeance, thanksgiving, confusion, betrayal, suffering. The Psalms are the Christian's prayer book.

Why Start With Adoration

"I will extol you, my God and King, and bless your name forever and ever... Great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised, and his greatness is unsearchable."

Psalm 145:1, 3

When the disciples asked how to pray, Jesus didn't start with needs. He started with adoration: "Our Father in heaven, hallowed be thy name." Your name is holy, God: you are Creator, we are creatures. Human instinct runs the opposite direction, straight to requests, worries, and complaints, and God genuinely welcomes those (Scripture tells us to cast our cares on Him). But prayer that never begins with praise eventually shrinks small. It's like sailing a boat while staring only at the deck instead of lifting your eyes to the horizon. Psalm 145 lifts the eyes first. It expands your view of God before it ever gets to your list of needs, which is exactly why the old "ACTS" pattern, Adoration, Confession, Thanksgiving, Supplication, gets the order right. Start with adoration, and confession, gratitude, and requests all fall into their proper place.

Two Ways to Pray a Psalm

The simplest way anyone can pray Scripture is to pray it almost exactly as written, changing only the pronouns and tenses so the words become a direct address to God: "I will extol you, my God and King... Lord, you are great and greatly to be praised." One of the real benefits of praying this way is confidence: you know you're praying words God Himself has already given, even on days you have no words of your own. Pray this way regularly, the way a song you hear often gets stuck in your head, and Scripture's own language starts surfacing in your prayers even when you're not looking at the page.

Even when I don't fully understand how a line of Scripture is true, I can rest confident that it is true, and pray it anyway.

Some lines will genuinely trip you up: "every day I will bless you" (what if I miss a day?), or "the wicked he will destroy" (does that line up with how I picture God?), or "the eyes of all look to you" (what about people who don't even believe in Him?). Those aren't reasons the phrases are wrong to pray. They're problems of understanding, not problems with the text, and Scripture doesn't need your full comprehension to be true or profitable to pray. You should still seek to understand it more deeply over time, but you don't have to wait for full understanding before you're allowed to pray it.

Key Point

Those aren't reasons the phrases are wrong to pray.

A second way takes a bit more work: praying the psalm's themes in your own words rather than its exact phrases, bringing in your own life and other Scripture to flesh the ideas out. Praying through Psalm 145's opening this way might sound like confessing that by nature you were opposed to God, and yet because of Christ's redemptive work you can now say you truly will bless His name forever; it might mean asking God to turn a resistant generation toward Him, willingly, starting with your own household. Both ways of praying are legitimate. The first anchors you in God's exact words when you have none of your own. The second helps you slow down and make those same truths personal.

Learn to Pray by Praying

Two practical challenges follow directly from this: find a regular time of prayer with others, even if all you do is listen. That's precisely how anyone learns, the same way a child learns language by hearing it spoken around them, not by staying home until they've mastered it alone. And pray Psalm 145 once a day, every day, for the next week, in whichever of these two ways fits the moment. A few minutes daily adoring the Creator of the universe will change how you think, how you live, and eventually how you pray even without the page in front of you.

Prayer isn't first about extracting something from God. It's about knowing Him, adoring Him, and giving Him the glory that's already His. So this week, begin where all true prayer begins: lift your eyes, bless His name, and let His greatness, not your requests, set the tone.
