From the Psalms

Psalm 1: The Blessed Man, the Wicked Man, and You

Psalm 1 draws a hard line between a blessed man who never sins and a wicked man who perishes, leaving no third option. If that description doesn't fit you, that's not an accident. It was never meant to.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

Psalm 1 doesn't leave you much room to maneuver. It describes a "blessed man" who never walks in the counsel of the wicked, never stands in the way of sinners, never sits in the seat of scoffers — and it describes him in the perfect tense, as someone who simply never has done these things. Then it describes "the wicked," who are not so, and who perish. Two categories. No middle ground offered.

Most of us read that and quietly try to find a third option. Surely I'm not fully in either camp — I'm somewhere in between, mostly trying, occasionally failing. But Psalm 1 doesn't build you an in-between. And once you see why, this psalm stops being an intimidating checklist and becomes something much better: a description of Jesus, and an invitation for you to be found in him.

A Psalm With No Third Option

Psalm 1 and Psalm 2 together were placed at the front of the whole book on purpose, as an introduction that teaches you how to read everything that follows. And what they teach first is that all of humanity divides into two categories, not three — a pattern that runs through the whole Bible, from the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil in Eden, to Moses setting blessing against curse at the end of Deuteronomy, to Joshua's call to choose. Read the psalm itself:

"Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked, nor stands in the way of sinners, nor sits in the seat of scoffers; but his delight is in the law of the Lord, and on his law he meditates day and night. He is like a tree planted by streams of water that yields its fruit in its season, and its leaf does not wither. In all that he does, he prospers. The wicked are not so, but are like chaff that the wind drives away."

Psalm 1:1–4

Notice the poetry even in translation: the psalm opens with the word "blessed" and closes with the word "perish" — in Hebrew, the first letter of the alphabet and the last, bookending the whole choice being described. There is a way of blessing and a way of perishing, and the psalm gives you exactly two doors.

Why the "Blessed Man" Can't Be You, and Was Never Meant to Be

Here's where most readings of this psalm go wrong. People see the description of the blessed man and treat it as a to-do list: never take bad advice, never live like a sinner, never mock the things of God, delight constantly in Scripture. Try hard enough, the thinking goes, and you become that man. But look again at the verbs. This isn't written as a command. It's written as a description, in the perfect tense — someone who simply never has walked in that counsel, not once. None of us can honestly claim that. First John says plainly that if you say you have no sin, you deceive yourself.

So who is this psalm actually describing? The only person who has ever perfectly refused the counsel of the wicked, never lived a sinner's life, never mocked the things of God, and delighted perfectly and constantly in his Father's law — is Jesus. Read the Gospels with this psalm in hand and you see it everywhere: Jesus repeatedly withdrawing from the crowd's wishes, even his own disciples' wishes, to go be alone with his Father and take counsel from him alone. Jesus never sinning, in contrast to every other human being who has ever lived. Jesus delighting perfectly in his Father's law, abiding in him the way a tree abides by a stream — bearing fruit in its season, its leaf never withering, even through the worst suffering anyone has ever endured, because his life was rooted somewhere the drought couldn't reach.

If Psalm 1's description of the blessed man doesn't perfectly describe you, that's not a problem with your reading. That's the psalm doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

Verse 6 confirms it: "the Lord knows the way of the righteous." Not you, but the righteous man that the Lord already knows and walks with. And Romans 3, quoting the Psalms directly, removes any doubt about where the rest of us stand: "None is righteous, no, not one... no one who does good, not even one." Psalm 1 is describing Jesus. If we miss that, we miss the psalm's most important reading.

The Wicked Man, and the Doorway Out of That Category

If you're not the blessed man — and none of us, on our own, are — then by the psalm's own logic, you're the wicked man. That's a hard word, and our hearts resist it; something in us wants to insist we're at least a little better than "wicked." But the psalm doesn't offer a middle category, and pretending otherwise doesn't change which side of the line we're actually on.

Here's the good news that Psalm 1 doesn't spell out by itself, but the rest of Scripture does. Turn to Psalm 32: "Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered... blessed is the man against whom the Lord counts no iniquity." There it is again — "blessed" — but now applied to someone who has transgressed, whose sin the Lord chooses not to count, because that person has taken refuge in the Son. And Psalm 2, which forms the other half of this introduction to the Psalter, ends exactly where the invitation lands: "Kiss the Son... Blessed are all who take refuge in him."

Key Point

If you're not the blessed man — and none of us, on our own, are — then by the psalm's own logic, you're the wicked man.

This is the whole shape of the gospel compressed into two psalms. We are, by nature, the wicked man of Psalm 1, headed toward the fate described there. But when we take refuge in the blessed man — in Jesus himself — his righteousness is credited to us, and we're given the standing of the very man the psalm describes. Not because we finally managed to keep the list. Because we stopped trying to be our own righteousness and took shelter in his instead.

Reading the Psalm Again, as a Mirror

Once you've seen that the blessed man is first and foremost Jesus, you can read the psalm a second way — not as a standard to achieve, but as evidence to examine. If you have genuinely taken refuge in Christ, you should be growing to look more and more like the man this psalm describes, even while never reaching him perfectly.

So ask honestly: What has your counsel been lately — whose voice shapes your decisions, what you watch, what you read, what you let influence you day after day? Is there sin in your life that keeps recurring, that you've quietly stopped fighting and simply accepted as "just how I am"? When something in Scripture or in preaching challenges you, is your first response defensiveness, or a real willingness to search it out and change? And what do you meditate on — not abstractly, but literally: what comes out of your mouth when you're anxious, when you're grieving, when you're overjoyed? The Hebrew word for "meditate" here covers the whole range of human emotion, from the cooing of a distressed dove to the growl of a lion over its prey. It's asking whether Scripture is what surfaces in you under pressure, or whether something else is.

Look, too, at where your roots actually go during hard seasons. A tree with shallow roots spreads wide, searching anxiously for water wherever it can. A tree planted by a stream sends its roots down, deeper, precisely when the drought comes. Jeremiah 17 describes the same tree: "it does not fear when heat comes... it is not anxious in the year of drought." The blessed man's fruitfulness was never about easy circumstances. It's what he does — and whom he turns to — when circumstances are hard.

Which Man Are You?

Psalm 1 forces a choice it never actually asks you to make by force of will. You can't grit your teeth and become the blessed man; that road only leads to exhaustion or self-deception. But you can recognize which category you're actually in, and you can take refuge in the one man who ever fulfilled this psalm perfectly on your behalf.

If you can see real, growing evidence of that refuge — a life that increasingly delights in God's word, roots that go down instead of just wide, fruit that wasn't there before — then today is a day to celebrate what he has done and is doing in you. And if you look honestly at this psalm and see the wicked man instead, unrepentant, still perishing like chaff in the wind, then today is the day to stop insisting on a middle ground that doesn't exist, and take refuge in the Son while the invitation still stands. Read through the Gospel of John this week, in one sitting if you can. Ask someone whose life shows the fruit this psalm describes to walk through your questions with you. This is exactly the kind of turning the church exists to help you make, and not one you're meant to make alone.
