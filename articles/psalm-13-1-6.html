How Long, O Lord? What Psalm 13 Teaches About Waiting on God

Psalm 13 is built to slow you down. Its structure hides the point until the very end, and the point is how a believer responds to God in the middle of unresolved pain.

Matthew Schaefer · March 22, 2026

The Howling Psalm

Some have called Psalm 13 the howling psalm, or the psalm of how long — one of the most famous of the lament psalms. David never actually tells us what he was going through. It might have been Saul chasing him through the wilderness, or Absalom's conspiracy years later, or a season of sickness. We don't know, and that's exactly what makes this psalm so relatable. We've all been in David's place. We've all cried out, how long, O Lord, must I be in this?

How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me? Psalm 13:1

We live in a fallen world. Poverty, cruelty, sickness in those we love and in our own bodies. Even in the church, disagreements and misunderstandings. Our bodies are, quite literally, getting closer to death every day. Our very state of existence cries out: how long, O Lord, how long are we going to be like this?

A Structure Built to Slow You Down

In Hebrew poetry, structure matters — maybe even more than it does in English. Psalm 13 falls into three stanzas: verses 1-2, verses 3-4, and verses 5-6, each representing a stage of a typical Hebrew lament. Stanza one is complaint — God, you're distant, have you abandoned me? Stanza two is petition — God, please answer, please show up. Stanza three is deliverance — a description of someone abandoning himself into the love of God. Problem, petition, praise. That's the ordinary shape of lament throughout Scripture.

But David does something unusual with the pattern to make sure we notice stanza three. In stanza one, he addresses the Lord, then references his own despair, then names the enemy's threat. Stanza two follows the exact same order — address, despair, enemy. As careful readers, we should expect stanza three to repeat that same pattern a third time. And it doesn't. Franz Delitzsch describes the psalm's overall movement well: "This song, as it were, casts up constantly lessening waves until it becomes still as the sea, when smooth as a mirror, and the only motion discernible at last is that of the joyous ripple of calm repose." The break in the pattern is deliberate. It's the psalmist grabbing us by the shoulders and saying: slow down, this is the point.

How Long, O Lord?

Stanza one uses a fourfold repetition of "how long" across five lines. When we're suffering, it always feels like forever. God seems to move on an entirely different timetable than ours — we think in minutes and hours and days; God tends to move in years, decades, even eternity.

Consider Joseph, sold into slavery at seventeen, thirteen years as a slave and then a prisoner before God moved him into power. Consider Paul, imprisoned on a false charge for two years while a corrupt governor waited for a bribe (Acts 24:27), desperate to get to Rome to preach the gospel he'd been called to preach. How long, O Lord, both of them must have asked. God had a different timeline than either of theirs for making things happen, and he has a different timeline than yours.

Verse two lets us hear David's despair plainly: "How long must I take counsel in my soul and have sorrow in my heart all the day?" A modern diagnosis might call this depression. Our culture teaches us to bury feelings like this — stay busy, get distracted, scroll, watch something. David shows us a different way: bring it to the Lord. Be honest with him. In the worst moments of our lives, our emotions are not too much for him. Our anguish is not too heavy, our heartache not too poignant, our questions not too much.

Consider and Answer Me

In stanza two, David moves from complaint to petition, asking for two specific things: that God would "consider and answer," and that God would "light up my eyes."

Consider and answer me, O Lord my God; light up my eyes, lest I sleep the sleep of death. Psalm 13:3

To "consider" means to look closely at — David is asking God to turn his face back toward him and pay attention to what's happening. And to have his eyes "lit up" is best understood through Paul's prayer for the Ephesians: "having the eyes of your heart enlightened, that you may know what is the hope to which he has called you" (Ephesians 1:18). This is a prayer for hope — eyes that can see past present affliction to the inheritance secured beyond it. Without that hope, David says, he will "sleep the sleep of death," and his enemies will say they have prevailed over him.

Key Point

In stanza two, David moves from complaint to petition, asking for two specific things: that God would "consider and answer," and that God would "light up my eyes."

What are David's enemies, and what are ours? The ancient church spoke of the threefold enemies of the soul: our own flesh, the world, and the devil (Ephesians 6:12). We don't wage war against people. We fight the passions of our own flesh, the deceitful philosophies of our culture, and the devil's lies with the truth of Scripture. When we pray Psalm 13 today, we're asking God to enlighten our eyes so we have the strength to fight all three.

Trust, Rejoice, Sing — Before Anything Changes

Now to the stanza the whole psalm has been building toward. Notice something important: David's circumstances haven't changed. He's not suddenly delivered. And yet:

But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation. I will sing to the Lord, because he has dealt bountifully with me. Psalm 13:5-6

Three works of God, three responses from the believer. God is steadfast in love, and David trusts. God saves, and David rejoices. God deals bountifully, and David sings.

His circumstances hadn't changed. His trust had.

Steadfast love includes the idea of patience — God is "slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love." When did you first deserve judgment for sin? Long before you think. Romans tells us the wages of sin are death, and yet God did not give any of us what we deserved the moment we deserved it. Even Adam, told plainly that the day he ate the fruit he would die, was met instead with patient, steadfast love — delayed justice, so that sin could be dealt with fully at the cross. That is something we can trust in the middle of suffering, because we've already proven it true in our own lives.

Then salvation. Delayed judgment alone would still be bad news for sinners who could never pay what they owed — like leaving a starving man chained in a pit and simply tossing him food from time to time. Ephesians 2 tells the fuller story: we were "dead in the trespasses and sins... but God, being rich in mercy... made us alive together with Christ." He didn't just delay our sentence. He broke the chains and pulled us out. That's why James can say count it all joy in trials, why Paul can tell us to rejoice in suffering, why Peter describes being overwhelmed with joy in sharing Christ's sufferings — because when you see the salvation you've been given, the light and momentary afflictions get put in their proper place.

And finally, bounty — not just enough, but abundance beyond what's needed. Romans 8:32 makes the case: "He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all, how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?" If God was willing to give the single most precious thing that has ever existed for your sake, he is not going to withhold the lesser things you need along the way. In the middle of suffering, we can trust that God would not give us a single thing that isn't for our good.

Sing Before the Waiting Ends

David's suffering wasn't resolved by the end of this psalm. Ours often isn't either. But the pattern he leaves us is not "wait until it's over, then trust." It's trust now, rejoice now, sing now — because the object of that trust, rejoicing, and song was never our circumstances. It was always God's steadfast love, his salvation, and his bounty toward us in Christ.

The next time you find yourself asking how long, O Lord — bring that question honestly to him, the way David did. Then let it lead you where David's led him: not to an answer about timing, but to remembering what is already true, and trusting, rejoicing, and singing before anything else changes.
