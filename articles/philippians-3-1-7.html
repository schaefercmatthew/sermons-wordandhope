Philippians

Paul's Impressive Resume, and Why He Threw It Away

Paul had every credential a first-century Jew could want, and he moved every single one of them from the gain column to the loss column. What made him do the accounting that way?

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

Imagine you're driving to visit a friend you haven't seen in years. You've got the address loaded into your maps app, and off you go. Before you leave, your friend warns you: watch out for the gas stations near me lately, there've been some scams, don't trust the locals too much. You file that away and keep driving. A few hours later, you realize you're hopelessly lost — the map has been sending you the wrong way the whole time. Finally your friend calls: "You should've been here by now. Stop following that. Let me give you the real directions."

That's roughly the shape of what Paul does in Philippians 3:1-7. He opens with a warning about people who will twist the gospel and misdirect you toward confidence in the flesh instead of Christ. Then he points to his own life as proof: the things he once thought were assets turned out to be worthless for salvation. And finally he shows the one treasure worth losing everything else to keep — knowing Christ Himself.

Rejoice in the Lord, Not in Your Circumstances

"Finally, my brothers, rejoice in the Lord. To write the same things to you is no trouble to me and is safe for you."

Philippians 3:1

"Finally" here doesn't mean Paul is wrapping up the letter. He uses the same word again a chapter later. It marks a transition, not a conclusion: he's moving into a new application of the mindset of Christ he's been urging all along, and here it takes the shape of a command — not a suggestion — to rejoice in the Lord. Notice exactly where that joy is rooted. Not in circumstances, not in health, not in success, not in family, and certainly not in religious performance. All of those change. Christ does not.

To rejoice in the Lord means finding your deepest gladness in who Christ is and what He's done — not shallow cheerfulness, not pretending hardship doesn't exist, but the deep conviction that Christ is better than anything you could gain or lose in this life. When you face sickness and rejoice that Christ has forgiven your sins, you're declaring that Christ is better than health. When you face financial strain and rejoice that He will never leave you, you're declaring He's better than money. When you face rejection and rejoice that He has welcomed you, you're declaring He's better than approval. Paul had already modeled this earlier in the letter: imprisoned, he rejoices that Christ is preached; facing possible execution, he says to live is Christ and to die is gain. Even death becomes, for him, an opportunity for more joy.

We do not have joy in the gifts themselves, but in the Giver of those gifts.

And why does Paul call this repetition "safe"? Not because he's worried about his own safety — he's already faced imprisonment without flinching. It's pastoral safety for the church: hearing the same truth again and again guards them against deception and strengthens the joy that comes from trusting Christ. Think of a doctor repeating critical instructions to a patient, not because the patient is slow, but because the stakes are worth the repetition.

Watch Out for the Dogs

"Look out for the dogs, look out for the evildoers, look out for those who mutilate the flesh. For we are the circumcision, who worship by the Spirit of God and glory in Christ Jesus, and put no confidence in the flesh."

Philippians 3:2-3

Three warnings, then three marks of the true church. Paul's threefold "look out" — better translated "beware" — is not a gentle nudge. It's an alarm. The word Paul uses for "dogs" is striking: in Jewish culture it usually meant Gentile outsiders, but Paul is deliberately flipping the label, drawing on Isaiah 56:10, where the prophet calls Israel's own faithless watchmen "silent dogs who cannot bark." The real threat isn't outsiders. It's the Judaizers inside the church insisting that law-keeping plus Christ is necessary for salvation — people Paul calls "the cutters," those who tear the church apart contending for an abolished rite.

This isn't a warning about some far-off compromised megachurch. False teaching doesn't always look extreme. Sometimes it's the prosperity gospel promising health and wealth to those with enough faith. Sometimes it's relying more on tradition and heritage than on the actual word of God. Sometimes it's a teacher quietly redefining sin until the gospel becomes little more than self-help. Watch for anyone, inside your own church, who points you toward anything other than Christ alone.

Against that, Paul gives three marks of genuine faith. We worship by the Spirit of God — spirit-led, not ritual-led, shaped by what God's word actually says rather than by human tradition. We glory in Christ Jesus — our boast is in Him, not in our achievements, money, positions, or knowledge. And we put no confidence in the flesh — no hope in ourselves, because everything in us and around us, spouses, finances, health, will eventually fail, exactly as Psalm 103:15-16 says: we flourish like grass, then the wind passes over and we're gone. Christ alone does not fail.

Every Credential, Moved to the Loss Column

"Circumcised on the eighth day, of the people of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews, as to the law, a Pharisee, as to zeal, a persecutor of the church, as to righteousness under the law, blameless."

Philippians 3:5-6

Now Paul turns his warning into personal testimony. He lists out, deliberately and meticulously, every credential a first-century Jew could want: circumcised exactly on schedule, from the tribe that kept faith with Judah when others revolted, a Hebrew with no Gentile blood anywhere in his ancestry, a Pharisee trained under a respected teacher of the law, zealous enough to have persecuted the church himself, blameless by every external measure of the law. If the Judaizers want to compare credentials, Paul says, "I have more." He's not boasting to build himself up. He's laying a foundation to show how radical what comes next actually is.

Key Point

Now Paul turns his warning into personal testimony.

"But whatever gain I had, I counted as loss for the sake of Christ."

Philippians 3:7

"Gain" is financial language — profit, advantage. Paul had a lifetime of what he considered spiritual profit stacked up, and he deliberately moves every entry from the gain column to the loss column. What was once in the black is now in the red. None of these things were bad in themselves — heritage, zeal, education, achievement are all good gifts. But the moment they become the basis of your hope before God, they turn from assets into liabilities. The rich young ruler walked away sorrowful because his possessions mattered more to him than following Christ. Whatever you cling to in place of Christ becomes dangerous to your soul, no matter how good it looked on your own ledger.

Do the Same Accounting

Everyone has something sitting in their gain column — moral effort, wealth, education, reputation, family name. These things are good. But if they become the ground of your confidence before God, they become liabilities, however impressive they look. Paul's testimony is a call to do the same accounting he did: take every false confidence, move it to the loss column, so that Christ alone remains your gain.

This is the turning point everything else in the chapter flows from. Confidence in the flesh — whether rituals, wealth, education, or heritage — will always fail. Even good things become dangerous when they replace Christ. But knowing Christ is worth more than everything else in this life put together: eternal life, forgiveness, fellowship with God, a hope that will not fade. That's the treasure hidden in the field, the pearl of great price Jesus described, worth selling everything else to obtain. The warning is sharp. The promise underneath it is sweet.
