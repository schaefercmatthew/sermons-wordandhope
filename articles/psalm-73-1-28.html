Stop Doing the Math: Psalm 73 on Envy and the Portion That Lasts

Asaph says out loud what envy makes you think: the wicked have it easy, and obedience looks like a waste. Then he walks into the sanctuary and finds a portion that outlasts every gift.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

The Math You Run at the Table

Every November someone hands you the same advice. Count your blessings, they say, and write down three things you are grateful for before breakfast. Good advice, and Scripture commands thanksgiving. But sit at a table with people you see once a year, and something else starts running in your head before the gratitude list gets written.

Someone has a bigger house. Someone's children seem to have turned out better. Someone got the promotion, the new truck, the scan that came back clean. Without deciding to, you start doing arithmetic, weighing your life against theirs, and the math almost never comes out in your favor.

Psalm 73 is about that arithmetic. Asaph was a Levite who led God's people in worship, and he writes as a man who came out the other side of something. He gives the answer before he gives the struggle: “Truly God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart.” Then comes the second verse and the psalm tilts: “But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled, my steps had nearly slipped.”

Asaph Says Out Loud What Envy Teaches You to Think

He does not stumble over doctrine. He stumbles over what he sees when he looks at people who have no use for God.

"For I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked. For they have no pangs until death; their bodies are fat and sleek. They are not in trouble as others are... And they say, 'How can God know? Is there knowledge in the Most High?' Behold, these are the wicked; always at ease, they increase in riches."

Psalm 73:3–12

Asaph is not exaggerating for effect. He describes a man who lives as though God is not watching, and whose life keeps working anyway. Trouble goes around his house instead of through it.

This is the part that stings, because it is true, and you have watched it happen. A coworker who treats church as a joke has a year where everything lands. A family with no room for God has children who thrive and money that holds. Meanwhile you pray, you repent, you keep showing up, and the year is hard.

The question underneath is the one envy keeps asking in the dark: why should I keep obeying? What has it gotten me?

The Ledger You Think God Owes You

Asaph follows the question to the bottom.

"All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence. For all the day long I have been stricken and rebuked every morning."

Psalm 73:13–14

All in vain. He is not saying obedience was hard. He is saying it did not pay. He kept his heart clean and his life got harder, and he has begun to read his faithfulness as a bad investment.

Underneath that complaint sits a ledger most of us keep. If I do my part, God will do his. If I am faithful in this marriage, it will get easier. We carry that ledger and notice the moment it stops balancing.

I have carried it. When a prayer goes unanswered for years, the thought arrives before I can stop it: what was the point of all that obedience? That is a quiet accusation that God has been unfair, which is also a claim that I earned something from him.

He has the speech ready. He could announce to God's people that serving God is a waste. He does not. “If I had said, 'I will speak thus,' I would have betrayed the generation of your children.” He takes the doubt to God and keeps it off the lips of the weak. So bring your questions to God, and to a pastor or friend who answers from the Word, before you hand them to everyone who might believe you.

Then he tries to reason his way out, and it wears him out. “When I thought how to understand this, it seemed to me a wearisome task.” Three in the morning, the same argument, no answer. He cannot think his way out because the problem is not a missing fact. He is standing in the wrong place.

One Word Changes the Psalm: Until

Sixteen verses of struggle, and then verse 17 turns the psalm on one word. “Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end.”

Nothing about the wicked changes. Their houses are still full and their bodies still healthy. The only change is where Asaph stands. He has climbed to an overlook. The woods are the same woods, but from up there he can see where the trail goes.

The sanctuary is where God meets his people. His Word is read there, and sacrifice points to sin and mercy. It was never only a building. Jesus said he would raise the temple in three days, and John tells us he meant his body. God still meets his people when they gather. If you are tired of thinking your way out of a doubt, come in. Sing. Hear the Word. Bring the question.

From that place Asaph sees the end of what he had been envying. “Truly you set them in slippery places; you make them fall to ruin... Like a dream when one awakes, O Lord, when you rouse yourself, you despise them as phantoms.” This is no victory lap. God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked. God holds the end, and the end is certain. We measure a life by the middle because the middle is all we can see.

The light also shows Asaph himself. “When my soul was embittered, when I was pricked in heart, I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you.” He leads God's people in song, and he calls his envy beastly. Sulking at the goodness of God is not a mood. It is sin.

The Portion That Outlasts Every Gift

Then comes the best word in the psalm. Nevertheless. Asaph has just confessed that he behaved like an animal toward God, and God does not let go.

"Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand. You guide me with your counsel, and afterward you will receive me to glory. Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you. My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever."

Psalm 73:23–26

Notice whose hand does the holding. Asaph never says he held on. He says God held his right hand. Cross an icy parking lot holding a child's hand and the difference is obvious: the child's grip is not what keeps him upright. My salvation hangs on the hand that holds me, not on the strength of my grip this week.

Then he calls God his portion. Every tribe received ground in the land except Levi, and the Lord told them, “I am your portion and your inheritance.” Asaph spent the psalm staring over the fence at his neighbor's field. At the end he says the Levites got the better share.

Here thanksgiving finally belongs. Gifts can be taken. A body fails, an account runs dry. If your joy is tied to the gift, it falls when the gift falls. If your joy is in the Giver, you still have your portion when the table is empty.

One man's heart was truly clean and his hands truly innocent, and he was stricken all day and rebuked every morning. On the cross his flesh and heart failed, and he went into the slippery places so that we could be held. The third day showed his obedience was not in vain.

When the math turns against you this week, take the question to God instead of the crowd, and go where his Word is opened. Then say the psalm's last line for yourself: “But for me it is good to be near God; I have made the Lord GOD my refuge, that I may tell of all your works.”

He himself is the portion that outlasts everything you have envied.
