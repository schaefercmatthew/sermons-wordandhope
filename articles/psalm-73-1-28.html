Psalms · Envy, Comparison, and Contentment

Why Do the Wicked Have It So Easy? Psalm 73 and the Portion That Outlasts Envy

When envy makes you doubt that God is good, Psalm 73 points past better circumstances to God himself, the portion that outlasts everything you have ever envied.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

Around Thanksgiving you start hearing the same advice from just about everyone. Be thankful. Count your blessings. Write down three things you are grateful for every morning. That is good advice, and being thankful is commanded in Scripture. But then you sit down at the Thanksgiving table and look around at family you may see only once a year, or even less often than that.

And you start to notice things. Someone has a bigger house than you. Someone's kids seem to have it all together. Somebody just got a promotion, a new truck, a clean report from the doctor. We start running an equation in our heads about fairness, about who has it best. I don't think people do this on purpose, but it is a tendency we have, and quite often the math does not come out in our favor. The grass is always greener on the other side.

You have heard that comparison is the thief of joy. That is exactly what happens to the psalmist in Psalm 73. You might think you already know the fix, since it is Thanksgiving and we need to be more thankful. Psalm 73 gives a different answer, at least in part. Asaph does not get out of his crisis of faith by making his list of blessings longer. Something else happens to him, and it is better than a thankfulness list.

When envy makes you doubt that God is good, you don't need better circumstances. You need God. He is the one who holds you, and he himself is the portion that outlasts everything you have ever envied. Asaph gives the answer before he gives the struggle.

Truly God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart. But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled, my steps had nearly slipped.

Psalm 73:1–2

He is writing after the fact, as a man who has come through something and can say with confidence what solid ground is.

God Is Good, but as for Me

Truly, God is good. Asaph clarifies who he means. In one sense God is good to all of Israel, and in another sense God is good to everyone, but Asaph narrows it right away to those who are pure in heart. Inside the larger group of Israel, God has always had a remnant. Paul says in Romans 9:6 that not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel. So when Asaph says the pure of heart, he means the saints, the people God has called, the people God has cleansed, and the people God has kept. Hold on to that word kept. We will come back to it.

Then verse 2 starts with but. Have you ever heard someone use that word to undo what they just said? Dinner was delicious, but lunch. They never finish the sentence, and you already know how they felt about lunch. God is good to Israel, but as for me. Asaph does not need to finish that sentence either.

In one sense he is confessing what Paul says in Romans 3:10, that none is righteous, no, not one. The bigger point is that Asaph is having a crisis of faith. His feet had almost stumbled in his belief that God is good. He is struggling to believe what he just said in verse 1. I think we have all been here. We struggle to believe that God has things under control, or that what we are going through could really be part of his plan.

What causes a man to doubt God's goodness like that? Verse 3 tells us what happened to Asaph. He was envious of the arrogant when he saw the prosperity of the wicked. There it is. Comparison stole his joy.

The Wicked Have It Easy, and Asaph Is Not Making It Up

Why do the wicked seem to be blessed when the godly aren't? We have plenty of sayings for it. Crime pays. No good deed goes unpunished. Nice guys finish last. The good die young. The Bible is not silent on this. Listen to Job in chapter 21.

Why do the wicked live, reach old age, and grow mighty in power? … What is the Almighty, that we should serve him? And what profit do we get if we pray to him?

Job 21:7, 15

Job is saying what is right in front of his face, and the same question sits under Asaph. In verses 4 through 16 Asaph runs through the list. The wicked have no pangs until death. Their bodies are fat and sleek, which sounds like an insult in English but is a way of saying they are healthy and well fed. Trouble does not come to their house. It goes around it. You know somebody like this. Everything just seems to work out for them.

Even when they set their mouths against the heavens, people follow them. They say, "How can God know?" They live like God is not watching, and they seem at ease about it. Maybe it is a coworker who mocks church and never seems to have a bad week. Maybe it is a family with no use for God, and sports, money, and marriage are all going well. And the envy comes out. Why couldn't I be more like that?

Here is what makes this section of Scripture so hard. Everything Asaph says is true. If it were false you could wave it away. But it really does seem like the wicked prosper, and so he reaches his conclusion.

All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence. For all the day long I have been stricken and rebuked every morning.

Psalm 73:13–14

All in vain. He is saying his life is harder for having lived pure. The underlying assumption is that if he keeps his heart clean, his life should go well. Godliness should pay. Maybe we don't say this out loud, but we feel it inside. "Look, I served faithfully for years, and then a diagnosis came. I raised my kids in the church, and then they walked away. I've prayed about this for years and nothing has changed. What was the point?"

Whether we recognize it or not, the belief underneath is that God owes us a good life for our obedience. When we don't get what we think we deserve, that is when the crisis of faith comes. Each of us, when we check, has some expectation in our hearts for what following God is going to get us in this life. Asaph looked at the wicked and they seemed to be getting what he should have gotten, and he was getting none of it.

Asaph does something wise here. He seems to have a speech ready, that it is all in vain to follow God. He could have said it out in the open, and young and weak people would have heard him. He holds his tongue.

If I had said, "I will speak thus," I would have betrayed the generation of your children.

Psalm 73:15

There are things you bring to the Lord before you bring them to a crowd. If you are struggling, you don't owe the whole church an update on every doubt. Take it to God. He is not too small to hear it, and there is no emotion you have that is too big for him. Take it to a pastor, or to a friend who will point you back to the word. Don't hide your struggle. I'm not suggesting that you can't or shouldn't share with anyone. There is a huge difference between saying, "I'm struggling to believe that God is good," and saying that it is vain to follow him.

Then Asaph tries to think his way out, and it wears him out. Maybe you have been there at three o'clock in the morning, turning it over and over and getting nowhere.

Until I Went into the Sanctuary of God

Sixteen verses of struggle, and then one little word.

But when I thought how to understand this, it seemed to me a wearisome task, until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end.

Psalm 73:16–17

Until. Nothing about the wicked has changed. Their lives are still easy. The only thing that has changed is Asaph's perspective. Imagine walking up an incline on a hike through heavy woods, where you can see maybe twenty feet of trail in front of you. Then you climb to an overlook and see the whole valley. You can see where the trail goes, the dips and the mountains. You are in the same woods you were in two minutes ago. You are just looking at them from a different place.

That is what the sanctuary does. It is where God meets his people, where his word is read and sacrifices point to sin and to mercy. Asaph walks in with the problem unsolved. And the sanctuary is not just a building. Jesus said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," and John makes it clear he was talking about his body. Hebrews 10 says that through him we have a new and living way into the presence of God. But God still meets with his people when they gather. He still opens his word. He still sets a table for us. If there is something you are tired of trying to think your way out of, don't stay out in the woods by yourself. Come in, sing with the saints, hear the word, and bring your questions.

There Asaph discerned their end. The wicked are not on solid ground. The language in verse 18 is the same as verse 2. Asaph almost slipped, and he thought the wicked had solid footing, but God sets them in slippery places. Asaph is not gloating. Jesus told a story in Luke 12 about a man whose barns were full, and he died that very night. In Ezekiel 33 God says he takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked, so we shouldn't either. Sometimes plenty of wicked people die comfortable in their beds. The point is that God holds the end, and the end is certain. We tend to measure a life by the middle of the story, and the middle is all we can see.

The sanctuary also turns the light on Asaph himself. He is a pure in heart man, a saint himself, a man who leads God's own people in song. This is what he says about himself.

When my soul was embittered, when I was pricked in heart, I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you.

Psalm 73:21–22

Picture a goat standing in a pasture full of long green grass. She shoves her head through the fence to get at the grass on the other side, and her head gets stuck. She forgets the farmer who put her there and all the provision she has been given. She sees only what is on the other side of the fence. That is what envy has done to Asaph. He knows the right theology, and he has his head stuck in a fence.

He was sulking at the goodness of God. How often are we this goat?

Nevertheless, You Hold My Right Hand

Even though Asaph was a beast toward God, how does God respond? Nevertheless.

Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand. You guide me with your counsel, and afterward you will receive me to glory.

Psalm 73:23–24

He does not say, I held on to you. He says, you held my right hand. Back in verse 2 his feet had almost stumbled, and that is how good Asaph's grip is. It is a slipping grip. Verse 23 is God's grip, and it does not slip. Praise God that my salvation is not based on my performance or how well I can hold on. It was not based on Asaph's, and it does not depend on your grip, church.

Think of walking through a slippery parking lot holding a toddler's hand. Half the time the toddler is trying to get away from you. Even when he does grab on, his little grip is not strong enough. Who keeps him from falling down? Even when he holds on tight, the reason he is not running into traffic is your grip on his hand.

Remember that word kept? This is what our fathers called the perseverance of the saints. Our confession, in chapter 17, says that those whom God has accepted in the beloved, effectually called and sanctified by his Spirit, can neither totally nor finally fall from the state of grace, but shall certainly persevere therein to the end. How comforting is that? Asaph fell. He fell hard in his heart, and he sinned against his God. But he did not fall totally, and he did not fall finally. The confession says this rests on the unchangeable decree of election, on the merit and intercession of Jesus Christ, on the oath of God, on the abiding of the Spirit in us, and on the nature of the covenant of grace. This is what holds us, church, not your grip.

Jesus says of his sheep that no one will snatch them out of his hand. He prayed for Peter that his faith would not fail, in Luke 22:32. Paul says that he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion, in Philippians 1:6. So if God is holding me, why does my grip even matter? The confession answers that saints can fall into sin through temptation and through neglect of the means of grace. Think about what pulled Asaph out of this crisis. He went into the sanctuary. God keeps his people through means. The same hand that holds you leads you in the right way of living, and directly into worship. "You guide me with your counsel." What is that counsel but the word?

Whom Have I in Heaven but You

Verses 25 and 26 are my favorite verses in this psalm, and among my favorites in all of Scripture.

Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you. My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever.

Psalm 73:25–26

Asaph's circumstances have not changed. The wicked still look comfortable, and his life is still hard. What has changed is what he is focused on. His body is wearing out. But when the heart fails, God does not. He calls God his portion. When Israel entered the land, every tribe got a piece of ground except the Levites. The Lord told them, "I am your portion and your inheritance" (Numbers 18:20). Asaph is a Levite. Everybody else got a field and the Levites got God. And Asaph, who spent most of the psalm looking over at his neighbor's field, finally says that he got the better share.

This is where Thanksgiving comes back in. Thankfulness for gifts can only take us so far, because gifts can be taken. Family can die. Your body will fail. Bank accounts can run out. If your joy rests on a gift, then your joy drops when the gift drops. If your joy sits in the giver, then even when the gifts are gone you still have your portion.

We can still enjoy our gifts. Paul says in 1 Timothy 6 that God richly provides us with everything to enjoy. Receive gifts as gifts. But I am begging you, do not put gifts in the seat that only God can sit in.

Verse 28 begins the way verse 2 did.

But for me it is good to be near God; I have made the Lord GOD my refuge, that I may tell of all your works.

Psalm 73:28

The first but sets up a domino effect that takes everything away. This but gives it all back. He has made the Lord his refuge, and a refuge is where you run when the storm is real. Then comes the last line, that I may tell of all your works. This is the thanksgiving, and it comes at the end of the psalm. Asaph started off by hiding his doubt from everybody, and now he wants to tell the entire congregation what God has done. Thanksgiving pours out of a man who has found that God himself is his portion. Envy says, look what they have that I don't. Thanksgiving says, look who I have.

Asaph said, all in vain I have kept my heart clean, and he was wrong about that. But there is one man in history who did truly keep his heart clean. His hands were truly innocent. He was stricken and rebuked all the day long. On the cross his flesh and his heart did fail, and he cried out that God had forsaken him. He went into the slippery places so that we could be held. He took the ruin that belongs to us. And on the third day the Father showed that his obedience was not in vain. He is the pure in heart. He is the reason God is good to Israel, and the reason Israel includes you. He is the reason that when your flesh and your heart fail, the hand holding yours will not let go.

If your joy sits in the giver, then even when the gifts are gone you still have your portion.

So when you look across the table, or down the street, or down at your phone, and you feel the math turn against you, say with Asaph, whom have I but you? When someone asks you what you are thankful for, you will have an answer no one can take away from you. It is not a gift, but your portion is in God.
