Why Standing Firm in Philippians 4 Starts With 'The Lord Is Near'

Unity, joy, gentleness, and hope don't come from circumstances. Philippians 4:1-5 grounds all four in a single fact: the Lord is near.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

You've had the experience: you get a text that says company is on the way, ten minutes out, and suddenly everything changes. The dishes you'd ignored for days become urgent. The clutter you'd stopped noticing gets swept into a closet. Even an argument you were in the middle of with your spouse gets set aside — it can wait, because someone important is about to be here. Presence changes behavior. When someone important is near, we act differently than when we think no one is watching.

That is the picture behind Philippians 4:1-5. "The Lord is at hand," Paul writes at the very end of this passage — not far off, not uncertain, but near. And if we actually believe that, our lives cannot stay the same. This short passage gives four marks of what it looks like to stand firm in that nearness: unity, joy, gentleness, and hope.

My Joy and My Crown

"Therefore, my brothers, whom I love and long for, my joy and crown, stand firm thus in the Lord, my beloved."

Philippians 4:1

This is not the voice of a cold instructor issuing orders. Paul loves this church — he longs for them, calls them his joy. And the word he chooses for "crown" is worth slowing down on. Greek has two different words for crown: diadema, the royal crown of kingship (the crown we sing about in "crown Him with many crowns"), and stephanos, the victory wreath given to a winning athlete in the Greek games — woven from olive branches, parsley, and bay leaves. Paul uses the second word. He's saying: you, Philippians, are my victory wreath. You are the fruit of my labor.

There is no joy in this world like bringing another soul to Christ.

Think of any teacher who watched a student go on to do something remarkable — that quiet pride of "I taught them." How much greater to say, at Christ's coming, "that soul is here because I prayed for them, labored for them, pointed them to Jesus." This is why Jesus's command to make disciples isn't offered as friendly advice; in the Greek it's an imperative, an order to be obeyed at once. And the reward for obeying it isn't abstract. It's people. A brother or sister who becomes your joy and your crown when Christ returns.

Agree in the Lord

"I entreat Euodia and I entreat Syntyche to agree in the Lord. Yes, I ask you also, true companion, to help these women, who have labored side by side with me in the gospel."

Philippians 4:2-3

We don't know what Euodia and Syntyche disagreed about. We only know it was serious enough that Paul names them, publicly, in a letter meant to be read aloud to the whole church. Imagine sitting in that congregation and hearing your own name read out loud. But notice what Paul does not do: he doesn't take a side, and he doesn't give us the details of the dispute. Instead he pleads with both women to "agree in the Lord" — unity grounded not in personality, preference, or who's right, but in the Lord Himself.

Paul even calls in reinforcement — a "true companion," likely a leader in the church — to help these two reconcile. Sometimes believers need someone else to step in, not to gossip or stir up sides, but to usher two people toward peace. And notice how Paul describes these women: they "labored side by side" with him in the gospel, front-line co-workers, striving for the advance of the gospel together. Even the most faithful workers can clash. The enemy loves to sow division exactly where gospel fruit is growing.

If there is someone in your own life — in your own church — that you're at odds with, someone you avoid or criticize or struggle to forgive, Paul's word to you is the same word he gave Euodia and Syntyche: agree in the Lord. Don't sweep it under the rug. Their names, Paul says, are written in the book of life. If you will stand together with that person in eternity, surely you can find a way to stand together now.

Joy Tied to the Sun, Not the Seasons

"Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, rejoice. Let your reasonableness be known to everyone. The Lord is at hand."

Philippians 4:4-5

This is not a shallow call to look on the bright side. Paul writes this from prison, his life genuinely on the line, and in that setting he commands the church — twice, not once — to rejoice always. How is that possible? The key phrase is "in the Lord." Our joy isn't rooted in circumstances, health, success, or anyone's approval; all of that shifts like sand. Joy in the Lord is rooted in His character, His promises, His unchanging presence.

Key Point

Let your reasonableness be known to everyone.

Think of circumstances as seasons: some days feel like spring, full of new growth and promise; other days feel like the dead of winter, cold and barren. If your joy is tied to the weather, it will change as surely as the weather does. But joy in the Lord is tied to the sun, not the season — hidden behind clouds sometimes, but never moved, never changed, always breaking through again. This is exactly what Habakkuk meant: "Though the fig tree should not blossom... yet I will rejoice in the Lord."

Paul pairs that joy with something our culture actively resists: gentleness. "Let your reasonableness be known to everyone" — other translations render it gentleness, forbearance, graciousness. Picture a small child alone in a crowded, noisy place, overwhelmed and afraid. Now picture that same child with their hand in their father's hand. The crowd is just as loud, but the child's heart is steady, because the father is near. He doesn't remove the noise. His presence changes everything anyway. That's Paul's picture for us: you don't have to grasp, defend, or demand your rights, because the Lord is near, and His presence steadies your heart even while the world presses in hard.

What Would Change If You Believed It?

Stand firm. Be united. Rejoice. Be gentle. None of these are optional extras; they're the fruit of one settled conviction: the Lord is near. His presence is real right now, by His Spirit. His return is certain, at any moment. His promises are sure, and not one of them will fail. If we actually believe that, despair cannot master us, division will not define us, and harshness will not mark us. How would your words change this week, your choices, your relationships, if you lived every day as though Christ stood right beside you? That is not a fantasy. That is the reality Paul is describing.
