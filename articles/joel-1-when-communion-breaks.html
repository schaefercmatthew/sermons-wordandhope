Joel 1: When You Can't Fix What's Broken Between You and God

You could read Joel 1 as a bad harvest report. Matthew Schaefer says that's missing the point: the locusts emptied a table in the temple, and Israel has no way to set it again on its own.

Matthew Schaefer · October 12, 2026

A Bad Harvest Report Is Not the Point

You could read Joel chapter 1 like a bad harvest report. Matthew Schaefer says you'd be missing a lot of it if that's all you saw: a nation sad because they didn't get a good year's harvest, or even a devastating one. There's a question underneath the ruined fields that the chapter is actually chasing, and it's not an agricultural one. What happens when communion with God gets broken? What does that look like, and what does it take to get it back?

Hold that second question while you read, because Joel 1 answers the first one in detail and then stops just short of answering the second. What it refuses to do is let you rush past the loss to find out.

Joel Gave You Everything You Need, and Nothing Else

Look at what the opening verse tells you about the man himself: he's a prophet, speaking on God's behalf. He's the son of a man named Pethuel. The word of the Lord came to him. That's it. Depending on how generously you count, that's two or three pieces of information, and there is nothing else about Joel anywhere else in Scripture. No date. No mention of a king, which is normally how you'd pin a prophetic book to a specific decade. Scholars have guessed anywhere across roughly five hundred years, before the exile or after it, and nobody actually knows.

That's not a gap to apologize for. It's a clue. If you needed more information about Joel, the Bible would have given it to you. Since it didn't, you're not meant to focus on the prophet, or his biography, or his era. You're meant to focus on the message he was given, because that message wasn't written for one king's reign or one generation's crisis. It was written to land on whoever reads it, whenever they read it, which is exactly why it still lands on you.

Nothing Left Standing

So Joel doesn't ease into this. There's no introduction, no stage-setting. The disaster just gets dropped at your feet in verse 2, like a mic drop: pay attention, this is a big deal.

Hear this, you elders; give ear, all inhabitants of the land! Has such a thing happened in your days, or in the days of your fathers? Tell your children of it, and let your children tell their children, and their children to another generation. Joel 1:2–3

Then he spends the entire first chapter telling you exactly how devastating it was. Verse 4 names four separate waves:

What the cutting locust left, the swarming locust has eaten. What the swarming locust left, the hopping locust has eaten, and what the hopping locust left, the destroying locust has eaten. Joel 1:4

Four different Hebrew words for locust: cutting, swarming, hopping, destroying. Each wave finishes whatever the last one couldn't reach. Schaefer compares it to the Malloy family's corn this year. The wind came through, then another wind came through, then the flood came through, and at the end there wasn't really any corn left. Now scale that up past one field, past one farm. Joel describes what hit Judah as an army without number that came against the whole country. Not a bad season. A total loss, land-wide.

And Joel wants this remembered for generations, several generations deep by his own instruction. That's a strange thing to ask for if this were only a lesson about crop rotation or planting mistakes. You don't pass down a farming correction for that long. You pass down something that mattered more than the crop.

The Table Is Shut

Here's where the chapter turns. Look at who Joel starts talking to in verse 9, and again in verse 13.

The grain offering and the drink offering are cut off from the house of the LORD. The priests mourn, the ministers of the LORD. Joel 1:9
Put on sackcloth and lament, O priests; wail, O ministers of the altar. Go in, pass the night in sackcloth, O ministers of my God! Because grain offering and drink offering are withheld from the house of your God. Joel 1:13

He isn't talking to the farmers anymore. He's talking to the priests. If this were a story about income lost or dinner tables going without, the farmers would still be the ones in the spotlight. Instead Joel narrows in on the men whose job was to bring the grain offering and the drink offering to the house of the Lord. That offering was the way Israel kept communion with God, the regular act that kept the inner part of the temple open to his people. The locusts didn't just wreck the economy. They emptied the temple table. There was nothing left to carry in.

What the locusts actually took

It's not food that's missing from this chapter, even though food gets mentioned again and again. What's missing is the offering, and without the offering the table in the temple has been shut to the people of God. That's what's actually broken here: communion with God, because there's nothing left to bring him.

The locust didn't just wreck the economy. It emptied the temple table.

Sit in the Sorrow

So what's the right response to communion with God being broken? Not a quick patch. Joel never once in this chapter tells anyone how to fix the problem. He tells the priests to put on sackcloth and spend the whole night in it. He tells the elders to make sure this gets remembered for generations. He keeps widening the lament instead of narrowing it toward a solution.

That's the difference between grief that wants relief and grief that actually feels the weight of what's gone. One kind of sorrow just wants the discomfort to end, wants to get back to normal as fast as possible. The other kind stays long enough to understand what was actually lost. Joel is after the second kind. He wants Israel to feel the real size of a shut table before anyone starts talking about reopening it, because a people who shrug off a broken communion with God will shrug off the next one too.

Israel Cannot Fix This Themselves

Chapter 1 never hands Israel a way out. There's no instruction for growing a faster crop, no formula for a substitute offering, no plan for restocking the table on their own. The chapter just keeps describing the loss, generation after generation of it, with the table still shut at the end.

That's the real weight underneath the ruined fields. It was never going to be enough for Israel to try harder, replant sooner, or scrape together whatever grain survived. The table required an offering, and Israel's own resources were exactly what the locusts had just destroyed. Communion with God, once broken this way, could not be bought back by the people standing in the wreckage. The chapter leaves that need open on purpose, because the fix was never going to come from inside the disaster. It points forward to someone who could bring a perfect offering when nobody else had anything left to bring, someone who sets the table again not because Israel finally produced enough, but because he supplies what Israel never could. Jesus at the table restores what the locusts took.
