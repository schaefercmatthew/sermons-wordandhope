Joel 1: The Locust Plague Wasn't About the Crops

A locust army strips Judah's fields to nothing, and Joel addresses the priests, not the farmers. The real loss in Joel 1 isn't the harvest. It's what the harvest was supposed to put on the table.

Matthew Schaefer · October 12, 2026

A Bad Harvest Isn't Always Just a Bad Harvest

You've had a year like this, even if locusts had nothing to do with it. The thing you depended on got taken away, and underneath that loss sat a harder question about where God had gone. Joel 1 opens with a nation standing in exactly that spot. The crops are gone, the wine is gone, and the chapter is not really interested in farming.

Joel tells you almost nothing about himself. No date, no king, no clue about which invading army or which generation. Every other prophetic book you can open starts by pinning you to a decade: "in the days of Uzziah," "in the thirteenth year of Josiah." Joel skips it. That's not an oversight. It means this chapter was never meant to describe one bad season you could file away as ancient history. It's written to describe something that keeps happening, in every generation, whenever the thing people depend on to meet God gets stripped away.

The Locusts Didn't Leave Anything

Joel starts with the elders and tells them to pass the story down:

Hear this, you elders; give ear, all inhabitants of the land! Has such a thing happened in your days, or in the days of your fathers? Tell your children of it, and let your children tell their children, and their children to another generation. Joel 1:2–3

Before he says a word about what actually happened, he tells you this is a story worth keeping for generations. That's your first clue that this isn't a crop report.

Then comes verse 4, and it's worth slowing down on:

What the cutting locust left, the swarming locust has eaten. What the swarming locust left, the hopping locust has eaten, and what the hopping locust left, the destroying locust has eaten. Joel 1:4

Four different Hebrew words for locust, four waves, each one finishing off what the last one couldn't reach. This isn't one swarm passing through a field. It's total devastation, described on purpose so you can't picture anything left standing. Whatever this invasion was, it didn't leave scraps.

Verses 5 through 12 catalog the damage: the wine is cut off, the vine is laid waste, the fig tree splintered and stripped white, the wheat and barley gone, the fields "destroyed," gladness itself "dried up from the children of man" (Joel 1:12). If you stopped reading there, you'd walk away thinking Joel wrote an ancient disaster report. Keep going. Verse 9 is where the chapter tells you what it's actually about.

What the Locusts Actually Took

The grain offering and the drink offering are cut off from the house of the LORD. The priests mourn, the ministers of the LORD. Joel 1:9

Read that again next to the ten verses before it. Joel has just spent an entire section describing dead vines and shriveled fig trees, and the sentence he lands on is about a table in a building. Verse 13 says it again, even more directly:

Put on sackcloth and lament, O priests; wail, O ministers of the altar. Go in, pass the night in sackcloth, O ministers of my God! Because grain offering and drink offering are withheld from the house of your God. Joel 1:13

Notice who Joel is actually talking to. He opened with the elders and "all inhabitants of the land." By verse 9 and 13, he's narrowed in on the priests specifically. If this were a chapter about crop failure and economic hardship, you'd expect him to keep talking to the farmers, the vinedressers, the people who'd lost their income. Instead he turns to the men whose entire job was to stand at that table and bring the offering. The farmers lost a harvest; the priests lost their function, and Joel is far more troubled by the second loss than the first.

Here's why that distinction matters. The grain offering and drink offering weren't decoration. They were the regular, prescribed way Israel maintained its relationship with God: the daily and ongoing acts that kept the house of the Lord functioning as the place where God's people met with him. When the locusts ate every stalk of grain and every grape on the vine, there was nothing left to bring to that table. Not low supply. None. The offering wasn't reduced. It was gone, and so was the thing it existed to do.

What Joel 1 is actually about

The locust army didn't just wreck a harvest. It emptied the table that was Israel's point of contact with God. The crop loss was real. It was also the surface of something worse: communion with God, interrupted, with nothing on the table to bring.

Verse 16 asks the question outright: "Is not the food cut off before our eyes, joy and gladness from the house of our God?" (Joel 1:16). Joel doesn't ask whether the barns are empty. He asks whether joy has gone out of God's house.

The locusts didn't just empty a field. They emptied a table that was never meant to go bare.

Why This Gets Passed Down for Generations

Go back to verse 3 with that in view. Why does Joel want this remembered by children and grandchildren who never saw a single locust? Not because losing a harvest is instructive. Harvests fail all the time, and nobody tells their grandchildren about an average bad year. Joel wants it remembered because broken communion with God is worth knowing what it looks like, specifically so you'd recognize it again. A generation that has never seen the table empty doesn't know what it costs to have it full.

That ought to land somewhere closer to home than ancient agriculture. You don't need a locust plague to lose your sense of standing before God. You can lose it through a habit you never corrected, a bitterness you never let go of, a devotion you let crowd out, a sin you kept managing instead of killing. The outward wreckage will look nothing like Joel's. Your bills still get paid. Your pantry still has food in it. But the inward reality can be the same one Joel is naming: the place where you used to meet God, quiet, with nothing on the table.

The Question This Chapter Leaves You With

The hard part of this chapter is that it doesn't tell you how to get the table filled again. It stays entirely in the damage. The priests are told to lament, to put on sackcloth, to spend the night mourning in it. That's as far as chapter 1 goes, and it's worth sitting in that discomfort rather than rushing past it. Before you can talk about restoration, you have to be able to recognize what's actually been lost. Joel 1 makes you look at the empty table before it tells you anything about getting it full again. That's a different question, and it's the one Joel starts answering as soon as chapter 2 opens.

For now, the question this chapter leaves you with is simpler and more personal: when you look at your own life, is there a table that's gone quiet? Not whether your circumstances are hard. Whether the place where you meet God still has anything on it. Joel wants you to be able to answer that honestly before you ever ask what comes next.
