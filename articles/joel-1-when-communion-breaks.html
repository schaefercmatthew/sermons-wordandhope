Joel 1: When You Can't Fix What's Broken Between You and God

A locust army strips Judah's fields bare, and the thing Joel mourns isn't the harvest. It's a table in the temple that nobody can fill by trying harder.

Matthew Schaefer · October 12, 2026

You Know This Feeling Even Without a Locust

Something is wrong between you and God and you cannot name the moment it happened. Prayer feels like talking into an empty room. The Bible reads flat. You go through the motions of faith and nothing meets you there. You tell yourself it will pass, and some mornings you almost believe it, and other mornings you just feel the hollowness again and wish you could skip to the part where it's fixed.

Joel 1 opens on a nation standing in that exact spot, except their version has a face you can see: a locust army that left Judah with nothing. The crops are gone. The wine is gone. And Joel will not let anyone rush past the loss to get to a solution. He makes Israel sit in it, name it, feel the whole weight of it, before he lets the book move an inch toward repair. That refusal to hurry is the first thing you need to understand about this chapter.

Nothing Left Standing

Joel starts with the elders and tells them this story has to outlive the people who lived it:

Hear this, you elders; give ear, all inhabitants of the land! Has such a thing happened in your days, or in the days of your fathers? Tell your children of it, and let your children tell their children, and their children to another generation. Joel 1:2–3

Before he describes the damage, he insists it be remembered. Then he describes it:

What the cutting locust left, the swarming locust has eaten. What the swarming locust left, the hopping locust has eaten, and what the hopping locust left, the destroying locust has eaten. Joel 1:4

Four Hebrew words for locust, four waves, each one finishing what the last one couldn't reach. This wasn't a swarm passing through. It was total, and Joel describes it on purpose so you cannot picture a single stalk left standing. Verses 5 through 12 keep going: the wine cut off, the vine laid waste, the fig tree splintered and stripped white, wheat and barley gone, the fields "destroyed," and gladness itself "dried up from the children of man" (Joel 1:12). You could read all of that and think you're holding an agricultural disaster report. Verse 9 tells you otherwise.

The Table Is Shut

The grain offering and the drink offering are cut off from the house of the LORD. The priests mourn, the ministers of the LORD. Joel 1:9

Watch who Joel is talking to. He opened with the elders and "all inhabitants of the land." By verse 9, and again in verse 13, he has narrowed to the priests specifically:

Put on sackcloth and lament, O priests; wail, O ministers of the altar. Go in, pass the night in sackcloth, O ministers of my God! Because grain offering and drink offering are withheld from the house of your God. Joel 1:13

If this chapter were about crop failure, Joel would stay with the farmers who lost their income. He doesn't. He turns to the men whose job was to stand at the altar and bring the offering, because the grain offering and drink offering weren't decoration. They were the regular, prescribed way Israel kept standing in fellowship with God, the daily acts that kept his house open as the place his people met him. When the locusts ate every stalk and every grape, there was nothing left to bring. Not a shortage. Nothing.

What the locusts actually took

They didn't just strip a field. They took away the one thing Israel could carry to the altar to stay in communion with God, and left the table with nothing on it. The table itself is still standing in the temple. It's just shut, because there's nothing to set on it.

Verse 16 says it plainly: "Is not the food cut off before our eyes, joy and gladness from the house of our God?" (Joel 1:16). Joel isn't asking about empty barns. He's asking whether joy has gone out of the house of God, because the way back into his presence has gone with it.

The locusts didn't just empty a field. They shut a table that Israel had no way to reopen.

Why Joel Won't Let Anyone Skip the Grief

Here is where most of us would want the chapter to move faster. Tell the priests to plant something, scrape together what they can, bring half a handful of grain if that's all there is. Joel does the opposite. He tells them to put on sackcloth, to wail, to spend the entire night in mourning clothes. Chapter 1 never once tells anyone how to fix the problem. It keeps widening the lament, verse after verse, until the whole land is in it: the farmers, the vinedressers, the priests, the ground itself.

That's not Joel wallowing, and it's not Israel told to feel bad for the sake of feeling bad. There's a kind of grief that just wants the ache to stop. It wants relief, not restoration. Joel is after something different. He wants Israel to feel the actual size of what's gone, because a people who shrug off a shut table will shrug off the next one too. You don't recover a relationship you were never willing to grieve losing. The sorrow Joel calls for is aimed at God, not just at the loss. It stays in the wound long enough to ask what the wound means, instead of reaching past it for the nearest comfort.

That's also why he tells the elders to hand this story down to children who never saw a single locust. Not because losing a harvest teaches a good lesson about hard work. Because a generation that has never felt what it costs to have the table shut will not recognize it when the table goes quiet in their own lives, in whatever form that takes for them. You don't need a locust plague to know a table like that. You can lose your sense of standing before God through a habit never corrected, a resentment never released, a sin managed instead of killed. The pantry stays full. The bills get paid. And still the place where you used to meet God sits there, quiet, with nothing on it.

Israel Cannot Reopen This Table

Sit with what chapter 1 actually gives Israel to work with: nothing. No instructions for growing a new crop faster. No formula for a substitute offering. No plan for getting back what was taken. It ends in verse 20 with even the wild animals panting for water, the fire still having "devoured" the pastures. That's the last image you're left with. Not a resolution. A land, and a priesthood, with no way back to the table on their own.

That's the real wound underneath the crop loss. It was never just that the grain was gone. Nothing Israel could manufacture, grow, or save was ever going to count as the offering, because the offering had to come from outside what the locusts had already ruined. Israel could lament. Israel could mourn. Israel could not provide what the table required, and the table required something whole.

That's why this chapter points past itself. The grain offering and drink offering were always a shadow of something, a way of saying "we need to bring something to be right with you" long before anyone understood what the real offering would be. Joel 1 leaves that need wide open, because the answer was never going to come from a better harvest. It was going to come from someone who could set a full table when nobody else had anything left to bring. Centuries later, at a different table, Jesus took bread that wasn't grown by the people eating it and poured out a cup that wasn't theirs to offer, and told his disciples this was his body and his blood, given for them. The table Joel watched close is the table Jesus reopens, not because Israel finally brought enough, but because he brought what Israel never could.

You don't get communion with God back by trying harder at the thing that broke it. You get it back because someone else supplied what was missing. That's the whole weight of Joel 1, held alongside the empty table it refuses to look away from: the loss is real, the grief is right, and the fix was never going to come from the ones standing in the wreckage.
