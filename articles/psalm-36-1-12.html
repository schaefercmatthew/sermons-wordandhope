Two Hearts, Side by Side: Psalm 36 on Deceit and Steadfast Love

Psalm 36 shows two pictures side by side: the deceitful darkness of the human heart, and a love from God so vast it fills the heavens. We're asked which one we'll live by.

Matthew Schaefer · May 17, 2026

The Dirty Window

Have you ever walked into a room with dirty windows and not realized how dirty they were until you started cleaning a section of the glass? Before that, everything looks basically normal — you get used to the haze without even knowing it's there. Then you clean it, and there's a sharp line: one side cloudy and gray, the other bright and clear. Suddenly you realize the problem was never with your eyes. It was with the glass you were looking through.

Psalm 36 works the same way. The first four verses have us look honestly at the human heart — not just at wicked people "out there," but at the sinful condition of mankind apart from the grace of Christ. It's a dark, self-flattering, restless picture. And then, without any transition word at all, verse five throws open the shutters: "Your steadfast love, O Lord, extends to the heavens." The shift is jarring on purpose. David wants us to feel the full contrast between the darkness of man and the greatness of God — because until we see both clearly, we never really pray rightly, and we never really understand grace.

A Counselor Whispering From Inside

Transgression speaks to the wicked deep in his heart; there is no fear of God before his eyes. For he flatters himself in his own eyes that his iniquity cannot be found out and hated. Psalm 36:1-2

David personifies sin — it speaks. And notice where its voice comes from: not from outside, but from deep within the wicked man's own heart. Jesus confirms exactly this in Matthew 15:18 — we aren't defiled by what comes from outside us, but by what comes out of our hearts. The Hebrew word David uses for "speaks" is striking; it's the word normally used for a divine oracle. Sin talks to us as though it were a message from God himself — which is exactly the temptation of "if I feel it, it must be true." But this inner counselor isn't God. It's sin, flattering us: it's not that bad, you're mostly a good person, no one will ever know, surely God understands.

Notice, too, that David never says the wicked man is unaware he's sinning. He knows. He's plotting, deliberately, even while telling himself otherwise. C.S. Lewis observed that the psalmists mention "hardly any kind of evil more often than evils of the tongue" — the gossip, the lies, the stonewalling that even the most "civilized" among us still practice, all of it flowing from the same deceitful heart.

What the Wicked Do With a Sleepless Night

Verse 4 says the wicked man "plots trouble while on his bed" — and it's worth noticing where. Other psalms show us what a godly response to a sleepless night looks like: Psalm 4:4 says, "be angry and do not sin; ponder in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent." Psalm 63 shows David remembering and meditating on God "in the watches of the night." Two very different uses of the same sleepless hours — one turns toward repentance, the other resolves to keep sinning.

This is us, apart from Christ. Not one of us starts with the fear of God built in. By nature we are children of wrath, as Paul says in Ephesians 2. Our hearts flatter us, tell us we're basically good, tell us the problem is always someone else, and whisper that our sin will never be found out.

Reaching for the Biggest Things He Knows

Your steadfast love, O Lord, extends to the heavens, your faithfulness to the clouds. Your righteousness is like the mountains of God; your judgments are like the great deep. Psalm 36:5-6

David just spent four verses in the small, damp darkness of the wicked heart. Now, without warning, the subject changes entirely, and everything about the psalm's tone changes with it. He reaches for the biggest images he can find — the heavens, the clouds, the mountains, the great deep — not as systematic theology, but as a picture: take everything vast you can imagine, and God's steadfast love is bigger still.

Take everything vast you can imagine. His love is bigger still.

The Hebrew word here, chesed, appears over two hundred times in the Old Testament and may be the single most important word for understanding who God is. It's loyal, covenantal love — not based on the worthiness of the one loved, not coming and going with circumstance, but rooted entirely in God's own character and covenant commitment. Ancient Hebrew thought of three "heavens" — the daytime sky, the night sky of stars and planets, and God's own dwelling place beyond both. David is saying God's love fills all three. That's how big it is. And it's for you.

Key Point

David just spent four verses in the small, damp darkness of the wicked heart.

The Fountain of Life

Verse 7 begins the application: "How precious is your steadfast love, O God! The children of mankind take refuge in the shadow of your wings." Not just Israel, not just the especially righteous — the children of mankind, a universal offer. The image of wings is deeply covenantal throughout Scripture: a mother bird covering her young, nearness, warmth, protection. This vast, heaven-filling God is not distant. He stoops. He shelters. He's close to all who take refuge in him.

David keeps piling on images — feasting on the abundance of God's house, drinking from the river of his delights, "for with you is the fountain of life. In your light do we see light." That last phrase deserves a pause. God isn't one source of light among many; he's the source. Without his light we can't see ourselves, the world, or each other rightly. John picks this up directly: "In him was life, and the life was the light of men" (John 1:4), and Jesus stands at that same fountain in John 4, offering water so that "whoever drinks... will never be thirsty again." What David only glimpsed, we now see fulfilled: that fountain was opened at tremendous cost. The full weight of everything described in verses 1-4 — the transgression, the deceit, the plotting — was placed on Christ at the cross. The fountain was opened with his blood.

Continue, Lord — Don't Stop

Oh, continue your steadfast love to those who know you, and your righteousness to the upright of heart. Psalm 36:10

Notice David isn't asking God to begin something new. He's asking him to continue what he's already tasted — the abundance, the river, the shelter of those wings. It's the simplest prayer: God, please don't stop. God doesn't need to begin loving us; he already has, in Christ. He doesn't need to give us something we lack; he's already given us every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places (Ephesians 1). Just: Lord, let me keep experiencing what you've already given.

Verse 11 follows naturally: "let not the foot of arrogance come upon me, nor the hand of the wicked drive me away." David knows his own vulnerability to the very voice described in verses 1-4, and he asks God to keep him near the fountain rather than let that arrogance crush him.

Honest Recognition, Then Gazing, Then Prayer

The psalm closes with certainty: the evildoers "lie fallen; they are thrust down, unable to rise" — stated three times, with total assurance. The victory is already won.

Notice the pattern this psalm gives us for prayer itself. First, honest recognition of our own hearts — not wallowing in guilt, but telling God plainly what we are apart from him. Second, and this is the movement we skip most often, gazing — stopping before we bring a single request to let the size of God's love settle on us, the way we'd look up at a night sky and let its vastness press down on the smallness of our fears. Only after that gazing does petition become easy, because it's no longer the desperate plea of someone unsure God is good. It's the confident request of a child to a Father who has already proven himself faithful. Romans 8:1 says there is no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus — that is the answer to Psalm 36's prayer, the fountain still flowing, available to all who come.
