Why Did God Say He Hated Esau? What Malachi 1 Actually Means

Malachi 1 opens with God saying "I have loved you" and Israel demanding proof. The Jacob and Esau line that follows isn't about two brothers. It's about what covenant love costs, and who it was always for.

Matthew Schaefer · September 20, 2026

Maybe you've read it and stopped cold: "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated." If those words unsettled you, you're reading them correctly. They're supposed to stop you. But the verse isn't really about Esau, and it isn't really about hate. It's about a love that a stubborn, ungrateful people didn't earn and couldn't explain away, and it opens one of the strangest, most overlooked books in the Bible.

Malachi is the last book of the Old Testament, and outside of one line about tithing, most people have never read a word of it. That's a shame, because the very first exchange in Malachi 1 answers a question every honest person eventually asks: does God actually love me, or do I just tell myself he does?

A Book We Know Almost Nothing About, Written to People Who'd Stopped Caring

We don't know much about Malachi the man. Other prophets come with a hometown, a father's name, some detail that locates them. Malachi has none of that. In fact, the Hebrew word "malachi" simply means "my messenger," and some readers through history have wondered whether the book even gives us a personal name at all, or whether it's simply identifying its human author by his function: a messenger, nothing more. Later, in chapter three, God says, "Behold, I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me," the verse that points forward to John the Baptist. The same word sits in both places.

That's not an accident worth explaining away. It's the emphasis of the whole book. Whoever Malachi was personally, the opening verse doesn't dwell on him for a second: "The word of the Lord to Israel by Malachi." This is a message from the Lord. The messenger is just the one carrying it.

Malachi wrote late, probably after the temple and the walls of Jerusalem had already been rebuilt following the exile. That matters, because Israel wasn't suffering under judgment anymore. They'd had their second chance. And what did they do with it? They grew cold. Priests were handing God their worst animals, the blind and the diseased ones, while keeping the healthy ones for themselves. Worship had become an obligation people resented rather than a relationship they treasured.

Malachi's whole book is written in a style scholars call disputation, which is really just a fancy word for a courtroom argument. God brings a charge. Israel denies it. God lays out the evidence. Israel objects again. It's a back-and-forth that plays out six times through the book, each one a fresh count in the same long-running case: God against a covenant people who have stopped believing he actually loves them.

The Text: God Opens the Case

Here's the first exchange in full, the one everything else in the book grows out of:

A pronouncement: the word of the Lord to Israel through Malachi. "I have loved you," says the Lord. But you ask, "How have you loved us?" "Was not Esau Jacob's brother?" declares the Lord. "Yet I have loved Jacob, but Esau I have hated. I have laid waste his hill country and left his heritage to jackals of the desert." If Edom says, "We are shattered, but we will rebuild the ruins," the Lord of hosts says, "They may build, but I will tear down, and they will be called 'the wicked country,' and 'the people with whom the Lord is angry forever.'" Your own eyes shall see this, and you shall say, "Great is the Lord beyond the border of Israel!" Malachi 1:1–5

Read it again and notice the shape of it. God speaks first. He doesn't lead with a complaint. He leads with a claim: "I have loved you." And Israel's answer isn't gratitude. It's a challenge. "How have you loved us?" That's not a request for clarification. That's a people telling God, to his face, "prove it."

Why Israel Didn't Believe They Were Loved

Put yourself where Israel stood. Generations earlier, God had promised judgment, and it came. Exile, ruin, a temple in rubble. Now the walls were rebuilt, the temple stood again, but life still felt hard, thin, unremarkable. If this was what being loved by God looked like, no wonder the question came out sharp. "How have you loved us?" isn't really a question. It's a flat denial dressed up as one. Name one way, they're saying, because we don't see it.

That posture explains everything else Malachi will go on to expose. A people who genuinely believe they're loved bring their best to the one who loves them. A people who suspect they've been shortchanged bring their leftovers, and that's exactly what the priests were doing with those blind and diseased animals a few verses later. Cold love produces careless worship. It's hard to give your best to someone you've quietly decided doesn't care about you.

"How have you loved us?" isn't really a question. It's a flat denial dressed up as one.

God's Answer: Look at Esau, Then Look at Yourself

God doesn't answer with a lecture on his own faithfulness. He answers with a comparison: "Was not Esau Jacob's brother? Yet I have loved Jacob, but Esau I have hated." And then, notice what he actually points to as proof. He doesn't say, "I gave Jacob the inheritance and withheld it from Esau." He says he laid Esau's hill country waste and left it to jackals. When Edom, the nation descended from Esau, vows to rebuild what was destroyed, God says flatly: go ahead and try. I will tear it down again. They will be called the wicked country, the people the Lord is angry with forever.

This is where the passage gets easy to misread. It sounds, on the surface, like a statement about two brothers, and about God arbitrarily favoring one man over another before either had done a thing to deserve it. But look closer at what the text actually says. Every proof God offers is about nations, not individuals: Edom's land, Edom's ruin, Edom's failed rebuilding project. Jacob here stands for Israel. Esau stands for Edom. This isn't a private judgment on two brothers' souls. It's God pointing at one whole people's history of ruin and saying: that's what you deserved too, and it isn't what you got.

That's the real argument. Israel and Edom, both descended from the same grandfather Isaac, both under the same righteous judgment for the same kind of unfaithfulness. Edom got exactly what its sin earned. Israel didn't. Not because Israel behaved better. Because God said, "I have loved you," and meant it before Israel ever gave him a reason to.

What Paul Does With This Verse in Romans 9

The apostle Paul picks up this exact line in Romans 9, and it's worth slowing down here, because Paul's argument only works if you've understood Malachi's the way we just did. Paul writes:

Though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad, in order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of him who calls, she was told, "The older will serve the younger." As it is written, "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated." What shall we say then? Is there injustice on God's part? By no means! Romans 9:11–14

Paul isn't claiming God arbitrarily loved one brother and despised the other as private individuals, with no bearing on either man's actual life or character. He's using Malachi's own point, that Israel's inheritance had nothing to do with Israel's merit, to make a bigger claim: God's mercy has never run on a payment system. It runs on his own choosing. "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy," God tells Moses. Not earned. Given.

And Paul takes it one step further than Malachi's original audience could have imagined. If Israel's inheritance was never about Israel's bloodline being special, then that inheritance was never sealed off to Israelites by birth in the first place. Paul says plainly that not everyone descended from Israel belongs to Israel; it's the children of promise, not merely the children of flesh, who are counted as offspring. Anyone who trusts in Jesus Christ, Jew or Gentile, is grafted into the same promise Malachi is defending in chapter one. The name "Israel" started as one man's name, became a nation's name, and now, through Christ, the true Israel, becomes the name for everyone who belongs to him by faith.

Edom's Silence Is the Proof

Here's a detail that should stop you: the Edomites don't exist anymore. Not as a distinct people, not as a nation, not even as a recognizable ethnic group. They were absorbed, intermarried, and scattered until nothing distinguishable was left. Everything we know about them comes from Scripture and old records, not from any living descendants who could tell you their own story. What God pronounced in Malachi 1:4, that Edom would try to rebuild and God would tear it down again, that they'd be called the people the Lord is angry with forever, wasn't rhetoric. It happened. It's still true.

The point of the comparison

Malachi 1 isn't proving how much God hated Esau. It's proving how much God loved Israel. Edom's judgment shows what everyone actually deserves. Israel's survival shows what mercy actually looks like.

That's the weight behind the last line of the passage: "Your own eyes shall see this, and you shall say, 'Great is the Lord beyond the border of Israel!'" Israel gets to watch this play out and draw the obvious conclusion. If God is this decisive in judgment, and Israel is still standing, still worshiping, still carrying the promise that would produce the Messiah, then Israel's survival was never a credit to Israel. It was a gift from a God whose greatness reaches past one nation's borders altogether, out toward every people he intends to save through the one Jacob's line would eventually produce.

What This Passage Is Actually Asking You

Strip away the ancient names and you're left standing right where Israel stood. Have you ever looked at your own life, the hard parts especially, and quietly concluded that God must not love you all that much? That if he did, things would look different, feel easier, cost less? That's Israel's question in verse two, and it's worth asking yourself honestly whether you've asked it too.

Malachi 1 doesn't tell you God loves you because you're better than you think. It tells you God loves you despite what you actually are. The proof isn't a feeling he owes you. It's a Savior he already sent you. A love that depends on your performance falls apart the moment your circumstances get hard. A love that never depended on your performance in the first place has nothing left to lose when they do.

What This Asks of You Now

If you've been treating God's love as something you have to feel proof of before you'll trust it, Malachi 1 hands you a different kind of proof. Not a feeling. A record. He didn't destroy you when your worship went cold, when your gratitude ran thin, when you brought him your leftovers instead of your best. He kept his covenant when you had already broken yours. Look at Edom, then look at yourself, still standing.

Stop asking God to prove his love the way Israel did, by demanding a better circumstance before you'll believe him. Start asking a harder, more honest question: given what I actually deserve, what does it mean that I'm still here at all? That question, taken seriously, is where trust in a covenant-keeping God actually begins.

The same God who kept his word to a cold, ungrateful nation kept it all the way through to a cross, where the debt Israel and Edom both owed got paid by somebody else. He already decided to love you before you gave him any reason to, and he has never once needed a better reason since.
