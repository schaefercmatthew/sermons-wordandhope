Which Saul Are You? 1 Samuel 26 on Mercy and Repeated Sin

David spares Saul's life for the second time. Saul says "I have sinned" for at least the third time. Same words, same tears, and it still leads nowhere. Here's the difference between sorrow that saves and sorrow that doesn't.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

In life, we often find ourselves facing the same trial over and over. You work through a conflict with your spouse, only to be hurt the same way again. You finally climb out of a season of financial pressure, only to find the budget tight again a year later. If you've ever tried to get into shape, you know the feeling of doing what seems like the exact same workout, week after week — same weights, same runs, same motions. And yet over time something does change. Not the routine. You. Your muscles grow. Your endurance builds. What looked repetitive was actually building strength you couldn't see happening.

That's exactly where we find David in 1 Samuel 26. We've seen this scene before: Saul hunting David, David given the chance to strike him down, David refusing to lift his hand against the Lord's anointed. The setting is different this time, and the temptation is quieter, but it's the same test, and this time, David passes it more completely than ever, because God has been using this same trial, again and again, to shape him into someone who looks more and more like the King who was still to come.

The Same Temptation, Answered More Completely

Betrayed again by the Ziphites, Saul marches 3,000 men into the wilderness of Ziph to hunt David down. David, learning of it, sneaks into the heart of Saul's own camp at night with Abishai. He finds Saul asleep, his spear stuck in the ground beside his head, the entire army in a supernatural deep sleep. It looks, by every earthly signal, like God has finally handed Saul over. Abishai even says so: "God has given your enemy into your hand this day" (1 Samuel 26:8), pleading for one strike.

"But David said to Abishai, 'Do not destroy him, for who can put out his hand against the Lord's anointed and be guiltless?'... The Lord forbid that I should put out my hand against the Lord's anointed.'"

1 Samuel 26:9-11

What's worth noticing is that this isn't David's first time facing exactly this test. Two chapters earlier, in the cave at En-gedi, David had a nearly identical opportunity and merely cut a corner off Saul's robe — a small act, but one his own conscience convicted him over, because it still carried a flicker of pride, a desire to prove something to himself. And the chapter after that, David came within a hairsbreadth of slaughtering Nabal's entire household in a fit of vengeance, spared only by Abigail's intervention. David is not a finished man in this story. He is a man under construction, being sanctified one repeated trial at a time. And here, in this third and final wilderness test, we see the difference all that sanctifying has made: David doesn't just refrain from killing Saul, he takes the spear and the water jar and leaves, entrusting Saul's fate entirely to God's justice rather than his own hand.

David is not the same man he was. He trusts more, and he reacts less. He has grown, most of all, in mercy.

That's what growth in godliness actually looks like. Not just sharper convictions or more knowledge, but a heart that increasingly bends toward mercy under pressure rather than away from it. God doesn't always remove a trial the first time it appears. Sometimes He brings you back to the same kind of test, again and again, precisely because that repetition is what produces the endurance James describes — a faith tested until it becomes mature and complete, lacking nothing. David is shaped by his suffering into someone who resembles Christ; Jesus himself needed no such shaping, since He was already perfectly righteous, but both point to the same mercy: the strength to answer an enemy's threat not with vengeance, but with restraint entrusted to a righteous Judge.

Two Sauls, Two Kinds of Grief

When Saul wakes and realizes what David has done — and hasn't done — he calls out across the valley: "Why does my lord pursue after his servant? For what have I done? What evil is on my hands?" (1 Samuel 26:18). It's a striking question, because centuries later another Saul would hear an almost identical challenge. Saul of Tarsus, riding toward Damascus with persecution in his plans, is stopped by a voice from heaven: "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" (Acts 9:4). Both men are pursuing someone innocent. Both are stopped mid-pursuit by mercy they didn't ask for. Both are humbled in the exact moment they expected to dominate — one by David's restraint, the other by Christ's glory.

But their responses could not be more different, and that difference is the real subject of this chapter. Saul the son of Kish answers David with words that sound like repentance: "I have sinned... I have acted foolishly, and have made a great mistake" (1 Samuel 26:21). If that sounds familiar, it should. It's nearly word for word what he said in chapter 24, and similar to what he said in chapter 15. His sorrow, to be fair, is real. Saul is genuinely sad in this moment. But it is the sorrow of a man ashamed he got caught, not a man broken before God. It recognizes sin without ever turning toward God because of it, and two chapters later, Saul is dead, having learned nothing from any of his confessions.

Key Point

It's a striking question, because centuries later another Saul would hear an almost identical challenge.

Saul of Tarsus, on the other hand, has his entire identity overturned by his encounter with mercy. His confession isn't "I made a mistake." It's total surrender, a complete reversal of mission and heart. He would later write the distinction himself: "Godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Same guilt, same confrontation with mercy, two entirely different outcomes. One Saul dies hardened. The other spends the rest of his life poured out for the very Christ he once persecuted.

Which Saul Are You?

That question isn't rhetorical. You have been confronted with the mercy of God, the same way both Sauls were. You and I have sinned against a holy God, and instead of the judgment we earned, He sent His own Son to bear our guilt, suffer in our place, and rise so that we could be made new. That mercy wasn't cheap. It was bought with blood, at the cross, where Jesus absorbed the wrath we actually deserved.

So how do you respond to it? Do you feel regret only when you're caught, then drift back to the same sin like a dog returning to its vomit — recognizing the wrong without ever actually turning? Or do you grieve because you have sinned against a holy God Himself, and run toward Him in repentance rather than away from Him in shame? Only Christ produces the kind of sorrow that actually leads somewhere. Mercy has come to you today, not because you've earned it any more than Saul had, but because Christ offers it freely. The only real question left is how you'll answer.
