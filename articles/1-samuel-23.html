What Does the Bible Say About Being Rejected for Doing Good? 1 Samuel 23

David saves an entire city from ruin. The moment danger turns his way, that same city is ready to hand him over. It's a hard story — and it's a window straight into the life of Christ.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

Few things are more heartbreaking than watching someone you love reject Christ. Maybe it's a friend you shared the gospel with, prayed with, grew up in church alongside. Maybe it's a family member you've pleaded with again and again about the hope of Christ, only to have them shrug, change the subject, or grow cold. They hear the good news, and they walk away — not quietly drifting, but actually turning. "I just don't believe it anymore," they say. Or worse: "I don't even want to hear about it." And the weight of that rejection settles down deep into your soul, leaving you wondering whether your hope was misplaced, whether all that praying and pleading and patience was for nothing.

It's right there, in that particular kind of sorrow, that 1 Samuel 23 has something to say. David knows heartache. He knows betrayal. In this chapter he delivers a whole city from ruin, and the moment danger shifts toward him, the very people he saved are ready to give him up. This isn't just a tragic moment in David's biography — it's a prophetic one. David isn't merely a character offering us a moral lesson. He is a shadow of the Messiah to come, and his anointing, his rejection, his suffering, and his kingship are all signposts pointing forward to Jesus.

The Anointed Deliverer Who Wasn't Wanted

Keilah was a fortified city on the edge of Judah's territory, right on the front line facing the Philistines — a stronger, better-armed enemy who had been raiding the region's threshing floors, stealing the harvest right out from under a city too afraid to stop them. When David hears of it, he doesn't rush in on his own initiative. He inquires of the Lord first, is told to go, and — despite his own men's fear — obeys. He and his six hundred outcasts defeat the Philistines and rescue Keilah.

"And David and his men went to Keilah and fought with the Philistines and brought away their livestock and struck them with a great blow. So David saved the inhabitants of Keilah."

1 Samuel 23:5

Notice the contrast the chapter is building. While David is inquiring of the Lord and obeying what he hears, King Saul — Israel's actual appointed protector — is nowhere near Keilah, doing nothing to defend it. The moment he learns David is there, though, he suddenly musters "all the people," not to fight the Philistines, but to besiege an Israelite city in order to capture David. Saul's only concern is his own throne. David is scheming to save; Saul is scheming to destroy. Even in exile, hunted and homeless, David is acting like the true king, and Saul, sitting on the actual throne, is acting like something worse than the enemy he's supposed to be fighting.

David saved Keilah at the risk of his life. Jesus saved us at the cost of His.

All of this points forward. Just as David sought the Lord's will before acting and never moved apart from it, Jesus lived in perfect, constant obedience to the Father — every word, every miracle, every step in full submission to God's plan. David saved a city with a ragtag band of the distressed and indebted; Jesus saves the world through the same pattern of humility and weakness rather than earthly power. His obedience, credited to us, becomes the very foundation our salvation rests on.

Israel Rejects Her Savior

David inquires of the Lord again: will the men of Keilah, the very city he just rescued, hand him over to Saul if it comes to that? The answer is yes. They will. So David and his six hundred flee with nowhere in particular to go — "they went wherever they could go" (1 Samuel 23:13). The deliverer becomes a fugitive, betrayed by the very people he saved. It's hard not to hear an echo centuries early: "Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head."

The pattern repeats with the Ziphites, David's own kinsmen from the tribe of Judah. He has done nothing to them but live quietly in their region, and still they report his location to Saul — who responds, astonishingly, "May you be blessed by the Lord, for you have had compassion on me" (1 Samuel 23:21). Saul invokes God's name while working directly against God's anointed. That's not just irony. That's religious language being used to mask spiritual rebellion, and it looks exactly like the religious leaders of Jesus's own day, who used the language of Scripture and zeal for God to justify handing an innocent man over to be crucified.

Key Point

David inquires of the Lord again: will the men of Keilah, the very city he just rescued, hand him over to Saul if it comes to that?

"And Saul sought him every day, but God did not give him into his hand."

1 Samuel 23:14

That line could be lifted straight out of the Gospels. Jesus, too, walked among crowds who wanted him dead, and again and again, "it was not yet his hour." Jesus was hunted, mocked, betrayed, his name dragged through accusations he never earned. He came to his own people, John tells us, and his own did not receive him. That's the deeper subject of this chapter — not just David's humiliation, but a shadow of the greater humiliation of Christ, who bore rejection that was rightfully ours so that we could be reconciled to the Father instead of cast out ourselves.

The Remnant Who Stayed

David's six hundred men were nobody's idea of Israel's finest — described earlier as everyone who was "in distress," "in debt," or "bitter in soul" (1 Samuel 22:2), the people society had already written off. And yet these are the ones who make up the true remnant of a believing Israel: not the powerful, not the popular, but the ones who recognized God's anointed and followed him into the wilderness anyway, exactly as Paul would later describe the church — not many wise, not many powerful, not many of noble birth, but the weak and lowly whom God calls to himself by faith rather than status.

The clearest picture of this remnant is Jonathan — Saul's own son and heir to the throne — who finds David hiding in the wilderness and tells him plainly, "You shall be king over Israel, and I shall be next to you" (1 Samuel 23:17). Jonathan willingly lays down his own claim to the crown because he sees what Saul refuses to see and believes what the Ziphites and the men of Keilah would not believe: that David, not Saul, is the Lord's true anointed.

That's the question this chapter finally puts to you. Are you part of that remnant — following the true King not because it's easy or popular, but because you know who he really is? The world rejected Jesus first, and it will not think more highly of you for following him than it did of him. But if you share in his rejection, you also share in his glory. Don't be ashamed of a Savior who was despised so that you wouldn't have to be. Wherever the King is hiding, wilderness or not, that is where the kingdom actually is — and you will never regret standing there with him.
