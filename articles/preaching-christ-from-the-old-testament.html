Knowing Christ

Did the Prophets Know They Were Writing About Jesus?

Matthew quotes Hosea 11:1, "Out of Egypt I called my son," and applies it to the child Jesus, a verse that was originally about Israel's past, not a future messiah. Either Matthew is stretching the text, or something is actually there.

Matthew Schaefer · September 20, 2026

Open Hosea 11 and read verse 1 on its own. "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son." It is a memory. God is looking back at the exodus, at a nation he rescued and then watched turn to idols. There is no future king in that sentence. There is no prediction. There is only a father remembering a son who broke his heart.

Then open Matthew 2. Herod is hunting the infant Jesus. Joseph takes his family to Egypt and later brings them home, and Matthew writes: "This was to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet, 'Out of Egypt I called my son'" (Matt. 2:15). Matthew is quoting Hosea. He is calling it fulfillment. And by any plain reading of Hosea's own sentence, there was nothing there to fulfill. Hosea was talking about Israel five hundred years earlier, not a baby escaping a massacre.

So what is Matthew doing? That question matters more than it looks like it does, because how you answer it decides how you are allowed to read the rest of the Old Testament. If Matthew is simply borrowing a phrase that sounded good, then the entire Christian habit of finding Christ in the Old Testament rests on the same shaky ground he is standing on. If something else is happening, something rooted in what Hosea was actually saying, then the Old Testament was pointing at Christ before Christ ever arrived to be pointed at.

The Prophets Knew More Than They Understood

Peter tells us plainly that the second option is correct. Writing to churches who had the Old Testament as their only Bible, he says the prophets "searched and inquired carefully, inquiring what person or time the Spirit of Christ in them was indicating when he predicted the sufferings of Christ and the subsequent glories" (1 Pet. 1:10–11). Read that again slowly. Peter does not say the prophets were writing history that later turned out to apply to Jesus by coincidence. He says the Spirit of Christ was in them, giving them words about Christ's sufferings and Christ's glory, and that they went back and studied their own writings trying to understand what they had been given.

They did not fully understand it. Peter says as much: it was revealed to them that they were serving future generations, not themselves (1 Pet. 1:12). But not understanding fully is not the same as writing about nothing. A man can know he has been handed something enormous without being able to see all of what it is. That is exactly Peter's picture of Isaiah, Hosea, and the rest of them. They were not groping in the dark toward some vague future hope. They knew whose sufferings and whose glory they were describing. They just could not yet see how it would happen, or when.

That changes what the Old Testament actually is. It is not a record of Israel's history that Christians later reread with new eyes and found useful analogies in. It was written by men who already knew, however dimly, that Christ was the one they were ultimately writing about.

Not understanding fully is not the same as writing about nothing.

Four Ways People Try to Explain What Matthew Is Doing

If Peter is right, we still need to know how it works. Scholars have answered that question a few different ways, and Hosea 11:1 is the test case nearly all of them use, because it is the hardest one to explain.

Some say Matthew simply borrowed the words. On this view, New Testament writers reused Old Testament language for their own purposes and were not especially concerned with what the original author meant. This view is honest about the problem. Hosea 11:1 really is a statement about Israel's past, not a prediction. But it asks you to believe Matthew was a careless reader of his own Bible, writing to a Jewish audience who would have opened their scrolls and checked him. That is a hard thing to believe about a man building a case for who Jesus is.

Others appeal to a hidden, deeper meaning available only to the apostles by direct revelation, a meaning the text itself never actually discloses and no one else could have found by reading it. This protects Matthew from the charge of misreading his Bible, but it costs you the ability to ever check his work. If the true meaning sits outside anything the words themselves say, there is no way to test the claim, and no way for you to learn to read Scripture the way Matthew did. It only tells you that he got it right. It cannot show you why.

Still others say Matthew was simply using an interpretive method common among first-century Jewish teachers, a method modern readers should not try to copy even while we accept his conclusion because he was inspired. This is more historically honest than the last view, but it runs into the same wall. If the method has nothing to do with what Hosea actually meant, then the connection to Christ looks arbitrary from Hosea's side of the page, no matter how confidently we accept the outcome.

There is a fourth answer, and it is the right one. Matthew is not importing meaning or borrowing a phrase. He is naming a pattern that was already sitting inside Hosea's own sentence, put there on purpose, waiting to be recognized once the one it pointed to finally showed up.

What Hosea Was Actually Setting Up

Look again at Hosea 11:1, but keep reading past it. "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son. The more they were called, the more they went away; they kept sacrificing to the Baals and burning offerings to idols" (Hos. 11:1–2). This is not a stray sentence. It is the start of a story Hosea tells about a son God rescued from slavery and raised in the wilderness, a son who then turned around and chased other gods the moment he had the freedom to choose.

That story is not decoration. It is the shape of Israel's entire history, and Hosea puts it right at the center of his book because Israel's whole covenant relationship with God ran along this same track: called out, tested, and unfaithful. A son, called by God, who failed.

Out of Egypt I called my son. The more they were called, the more they went away; they kept sacrificing to the Baals and burning offerings to idols. Hosea 11:1–2

Now go back to Matthew. Herod plays Pharaoh, a king willing to kill children to protect his throne. Egypt becomes the place of refuge instead of slavery, but the shape is the same one Israel lived through: driven down into Egypt, then called back out again as God's son. Matthew wants you to see that pattern repeating in Jesus. The difference is what happens next. Israel, God's son, was called and went wrong. Jesus, God's Son, is called and stays faithful all the way through, obedient where the first son failed. Later in his gospel Matthew shows Jesus tested in the wilderness the way Israel was tested for forty years, and where Israel gave in, Jesus does not.

That is what a type is. Not a hidden code buried under the text, and not a phrase yanked out of its setting and stapled onto something new. A pattern the Old Testament text itself already sets up, real and visible to a careful first reader, that turns out to reach further than that first reader could see. Israel's exodus was a genuine, complete historical event. It was also, in God's own design, a shadow of a greater son who would walk the same road without stumbling. Christ does not override Hosea's meaning. He fulfills it, the way the last note of a song completes a melody that was already playing.

What makes this different from guessing

A typological reading only works if the pattern is actually there in the earlier text, not invented after the fact. Hosea already draws a straight line from Egypt to sonship to unfaithfulness. Matthew does not create that line. He shows you where it was always headed.

Why This Is Not Guesswork

This is harder than it sounds, and it should be. Anyone can force a connection between two unrelated verses if they are willing to squint. That is not what typology asks you to do. A real type has to be grounded in something the Old Testament text is actually doing on its own terms, confirmed by the way the whole story of Scripture eventually resolves. Hosea was not privately hoping someone would someday connect his sentence to a Messiah. He was describing Israel as God's son, in language soaked in exodus history that every Israelite reader would have recognized instantly. That connection between sonship and exodus was already load-bearing in his own chapter before Matthew ever touched it.

This is the difference between finding Christ everywhere by force and finding him where the text has actually gone looking for him. It takes real work to tell the two apart, and it means every passage has to earn its connection to Christ on its own terms rather than have one assumed. But it also means something remarkable is true of your Bible. The Old Testament was never simply Israel's story that Christians later found a clever way to repurpose. It was written toward Christ from the start, by men who, however dimly, already knew whose story they were telling.

What This Means for You

If you have ever read the Old Testament and felt like it belonged to someone else's history, a story about a nation and a law and a temple that has nothing left to do with you, this is the corrective. Every promise God made to Israel, every son he called and tested, every place he rescued his people from, was building toward a Son who would finally get it right. You are not reading someone else's story with a Christian gloss stapled on top of it. You are reading the story Christ was always the point of.

That is worth something the next time you sit down with a passage that seems to have nothing to do with Jesus on its surface. Ask what pattern the passage itself is setting up: a rescue, a king, a sacrifice, a temple, a shepherd. Then ask where that pattern finally lands. Usually it lands on him. Not because you decided to make it about him, but because the men who wrote it already knew, however dimly, that it was.
