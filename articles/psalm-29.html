What Is Your Baal? Psalm 29 on Idols and God's Glory

Psalm 29 takes every claim the ancient world gave to a storm god and hands it back to the true one. It leaves us asking: what have we been crediting to our own idols?

Matthew Schaefer · August 16, 2026

Weather, or Glory?

There's a quote making the rounds whose author I can't track down, but it's worth repeating anyway: "Today, men look up at the sky and see weather. In the days of old, men looked at the heavens and saw glory." We've all had a week of storms at some point — lightning flashing every second like it's the middle of the day, thunder booming under our feet, floods rising in low places. Psalm 29 says none of that is random. Every bit of it rightly belongs to Yahweh.

Here's the whole point of this psalm, right up front: the God of the universe made all things, rules all things, and even now has perfect control of all things. Because of that, he deserves all our glory, all our honor — every blessing we have should be poured back to him in worship, simply because that's what we were made to do.

The Only Fitting Response

Ascribe to the Lord, O heavenly beings, ascribe to the Lord glory and strength. Ascribe to the Lord the glory due his name. Worship the Lord in the splendor of holiness. Psalm 29:1-2

Think about standing near a campfire. You don't have to reason your way into feeling its warmth — you just feel it. It's not optional, it's simply what fire does to anyone near it. Worship works the same way. It isn't begrudging duty or box-checking because God said so. When you actually see God for who he is — creator, sustainer, the one who thunders over the waters — worship is the only fitting response. Not because of what he's done for you personally, though he's done plenty, but simply because of who he is.

Stripping Baal's Resume

Israel needed this reminder constantly. They were a stiff-necked people who kept turning from God's goodness and then complaining about him. First Peter tells us the prophets wrote for us too — so if Israel needed the reminder, so do we, even when we can explain the theology of idolatry backward and forward while our hearts keep wandering.

Watch how this played out. Baal, sometimes called Baal Hadad, was the Canaanites' storm god — the "rider of the clouds," lord of thunder, believed to send the rain that fed their crops and their children. Scholars who study this region point out that Psalm 29 uses language strikingly close to how the Canaanites praised Baal. Count the phrase "the voice of the Lord" in this psalm — it appears seven times, echoing Canaanite texts that describe Baal's sevenfold thunder. It's as if David took Baal's resume, the storms, the thunder, the power over the waters, and, line by line, stripped it off Baal and laid it at Yahweh's feet. No, David says. That was never Baal. It was always the Lord.

Scholars debate whether this psalm was written as a deliberate polemic or simply borrowed familiar language later — I won't pretend that's settled. What isn't disputed is that the resemblance is real, and in Psalm 29, whatever claim the Canaanites or Israelites gave Baal for the storm gets stripped away and returned to the true God.

A Pattern That Runs Through Scripture

This isn't the only place God contends with false gods and comes out on top. Think of the ten plagues — many scholars see each one as a direct challenge to a specific Egyptian deity: the Nile turned to blood confronting the fertility gods, frogs mocking the frog-headed goddess of birth, darkness challenging Ra, the sun god. After it was over, Moses's father-in-law Jethro said, "now I know that the Lord is greater than all gods" (Exodus 18:11). Deuteronomy 10:17 calls him "God of gods and Lord of lords." Even Colossians 2:15 says Christ disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame by triumphing over them. Every time, the true God comes out on top, because he's the only real one. Everything else is an idol.

And still Israel turned back to Baal. Hosea 2 pictures Israel saying, "I will go after my lovers who give me my bread and my water, my wool and my flax, my oil and my drink" — crediting Baal for gifts God had given. God's response: "she did not know that it was I who gave her the grain, the wine, the oil." They took his gifts and melted them into idols. After the Red Sea, after manna for forty years, after fire falling from heaven — and still wondering whether some storm god might bless their children instead.

Key Point

This isn't the only place God contends with false gods and comes out on top.

What's Your Baal?

The human heart is an idol factory.

Calvin's famous line still lands: "We're constantly finding new things to worship that aren't God." Psalm 29 isn't just an interesting ancient polemic. It's asking you directly, what is your Baal? What are you crediting for rain in your life that actually belongs to the God who sends it?

David's method here is instructive: he ran an audit, taking what Israel credited to Baal and laying it, line by line, at Yahweh's feet instead. Thankfulness alone isn't a reliable test for idolatry — you can thank God for the paycheck with your mouth and still be asking your career to hold you together and give you your identity. A better test, from Tim Keller's Counterfeit Gods: an idol is whatever you look to and say in your heart that if you have it, your life will have meaning, value, and security. It usually shows up in what you cannot lose without despair.

Consider where control quietly takes the reins — family planning, a diagnosis in a hospital room, a budget that doesn't add up at the end of the month. Notice whether what rises in you is wisdom and stewardship, or dread and a tightening chest that says it all rests on me. Consider the weather app checked before prayer, the GPS trusted without question while asking God to order your steps goes unmentioned, the symptom search at eleven at night. None of these are sin by themselves. But where does your soul turn first for comfort — the scroll, the drink, the show, the snack — before a single word of prayer? That reflex tells you where your soul actually rests. God is supposed to be the reflex. For many of us, honestly, he's the fallback.

The Storm That Fell on Christ Instead

The Lord sits enthroned over the flood. The Lord sits enthroned as king forever. May the Lord give strength to his people. May the Lord bless his people with peace. Psalm 29:10-11

If the audit stopped there, all I'd have handed you is one more thing to fail at. That's law, not gospel. So look back at the psalm itself. For nine verses it's genuinely terrifying: the voice of the Lord breaking cedars, flashing fire, shaking the wilderness, stripping forests bare. That's not comfort. That's judgment, and honestly, it's what we deserve for every idol we've ever bowed to instead of God.

But look where it lands. After all that thunder and breaking and shaking, verse 11 says: "May the Lord give strength to his people and may the Lord bless his people with peace." Where does that strength and peace come from, right on the other side of judgment like that? Here's the answer the whole Bible points to: the storm that should have fallen on us fell on Jesus instead. He stood under the full voice of the Lord, the full weight of judgment, in our place, on the cross. The thunder that should have broken us broke him. And because it did, what reaches you today isn't judgment. It's strength and peace, freely given, not earned.

Run the Audit, Then Run to the Cross

Running that audit on your own heart isn't an invitation to fix yourself before you're welcome here. It's an invitation to bring what you find straight to the cross, because that's the only place idols lose their grip. You don't tear one down by trying harder. You tear it down by seeing what it promises — security, comfort, control — and recognizing Christ has already purchased that for you at a price you could never afford.

The Lord gives strength to his people not because we've earned it by finding and dismantling every idol ourselves, but because he already bore the judgment those idols deserved. All that's left for you is to receive strength and peace, forever. What is your Baal today? Will you finally lay it at his feet instead?
