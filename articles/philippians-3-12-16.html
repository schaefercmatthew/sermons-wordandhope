Not There Yet: Pressing On in Philippians 3:12-16

Paul, the apostle who wrote half the New Testament, flatly refuses to say he has arrived. Philippians 3:12-16 on why the Christian life is a race run to the very end.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

At twenty-two, I decided to run my first half marathon. I hadn't trained enough. I was twenty-two, and I figured it would be easy, but I even set a goal finish time, confident I'd hit it. For the first nine miles, everything went well. My pace was good, the crowd was cheering, and I felt strong. Then at mile nine my body hit a wall. Every step got heavy. My lungs burned. My legs begged me to stop. I started looking for an out, and I found one: my car was parked in a lot just past some woods to my right. I actually stepped off the race course and started walking toward it.

About fifteen steps into the woods, a race official spotted me and called out, thinking I was trying to cheat. I panicked, told them I just needed the restroom, and reluctantly walked back onto the course. And once I was back on it, I pushed through the fatigue and the self-doubt and finished the race, faster than my original goal time. I had genuinely tried to quit, and I was called back onto the track.

Paul plays the role of that race official in Philippians 3:12-16. He has spent this chapter warning us not to trust our own righteousness, not to boast in religious accomplishment, not to rest as though we've already finished. Now he presses further: the Christian life isn't about arriving early or resting on past victories. It's about running the race with your eyes fixed on Christ, all the way to the end.

Not Yet Perfect, and He Knows It

"Not that I have already obtained this or am already perfect, but I press on to make it my own, because Christ Jesus has made me his own."

Philippians 3:12

Given how much of the New Testament Paul wrote, and with what spiritual clarity, you might expect him to claim he'd basically arrived. He does the opposite. There is zero perfectionism in Paul. Calvin observed that true Christian maturity is not the claim to have reached perfection, but the honest recognition of how far you still have to go. Contrast that with so much of what passes for Christian leadership today: an image carefully built to say "I've already made it, I don't struggle." That posture is dangerous, because it discourages everyone watching, who assumes real maturity means the struggle stops.

As Baptists say "once saved, always saved," and there is real truth in that: salvation is secure in Christ, and no one snatches a sheep from His hand. But if we're not careful, that phrase can make salvation sound like something purely in the past, finished the day we prayed a prayer or got baptized. Scripture actually speaks of salvation in three tenses at once. You have been saved (past). You are being saved (present). You will be saved (future). Paul tells the Corinthians exactly this in 1 Corinthians 15:1-2: they received the gospel, they stand in it now, and they are being saved by it as they hold fast to it. All three are true simultaneously.

If we act like we've already obtained it, we will stop running.

That's exactly what happened at the end of my half marathon: the moment I crossed the finish line, I stopped moving for days. If we treat salvation as something completely finished, with no present struggle and no future hope left, we go complacent. Notice, though, Paul's motivation for pressing on: "because Christ Jesus has made me his own." He doesn't run to earn Christ's favor. He runs because Christ has already taken hold of him first. Christ has laid claim to Paul's life, and Paul gives everything he has to lay hold of Christ in return.

Standing at the Gate Is Not the Prize

"Brothers, I do not consider that I have made it my own. But one thing I do: forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus."

Philippians 3:13-14

Picture the five children who receive golden tickets to Willy Wonka's chocolate factory. They walk through the gates, shake Wonka's hand, and he says, "Come in, taste the sweets, see how it's all made." Now imagine one child says, "No thanks, I'm okay right here inside the gate." That would be absurd. And yet it's exactly what so many Christians do: Christ has opened the gate and invited us in to know Him more fully, and we settle for standing just inside it, never pressing further.

Paul refuses that posture. "One thing I do," he says, not ten things, not several competing pursuits. One thing: pressing forward. And that pursuit has two parts. First, forgetting what lies behind, not because the past doesn't matter, but because it can become a trap in two opposite directions. Sometimes we lean on past accomplishments: I've served faithfully, I've given, I've grown, and quietly rest there, as though yesterday's obedience could substitute for today's dependence on Christ. Other times we're haunted by past failures, replaying words we regret and decisions we wish we could undo, until guilt freezes our stride. Paul says: leave both behind. The past is a teacher, not a resting place. It's a warning, not a cage. It's a foundation, never a finish line.

Second, straining forward to what lies ahead. The word Paul uses pictures a runner leaning hard into the tape, muscles taut, eyes fixed only on the line ahead: not a casual jog, but urgent, determined effort. And the goal he names is "the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus": not comfort, not recognition, not achievement, but the summons to deeper fellowship with Christ, to being shaped into His image, to eternity with Him. It's a high calling precisely because it comes from God, not from us, and because it summons us toward where Christ already sits at the Father's right hand.

Hold Fast to What You've Attained

"Let those of us who are mature think this way, and if in anything you think otherwise, God will reveal that also to you. Only let us hold true to what we have attained."

Philippians 3:15-16

Paul shifts from his own testimony to the whole church at Philippi. Maturity, he says, is not measured by self-confidence or certainty about every doctrine, but by the humble, disciplined pursuit of Christ. And notice his patience: he doesn't demand uniformity. If someone sees a matter differently, God will reveal that too, in time. We're all at different points along the same road; the goal never changes, even when our understanding of every detail hasn't caught up yet.

Key Point

Paul shifts from his own testimony to the whole church at Philippi.

Then comes the practical command: hold true to what we've attained. Not stagnation, but disciplined perseverance: the picture of a soldier holding a position on the battlefield until ordered to advance. Guard the ground already gained. Don't drift backward while you wait for the rest of the road to become clear.

Are We Content to Merely Stand?

This question belongs to every believer, not just to Paul. Have you settled for what you've already attained, content with a familiar routine, a familiar circle, nodding along without going any deeper? That's standing at the gate. It isn't pressing on. Are you satisfied with the minimum: occasional prayer, Bible reading when convenient, service only when it's easy?

Scripture calls believers to press on with real effort, together: engaging God's word daily, not just occasionally; praying with real intentionality, not letting prayer become an afterthought; serving sacrificially rather than waiting for convenience; encouraging and building up one another; and reaching outward as salt and light to the people around you. None of that earns the prize. Christ has already taken hold of us first. But everything in this passage insists we respond to that grasp by running, not standing still. The race is not for personal glory. It is for Christ, who laid hold of us, and who will bring what He started in us to completion.
