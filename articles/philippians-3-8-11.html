Everything Else Is Rubbish: Paul's Verdict on Knowing Christ

Thirty years after his conversion, in a Roman prison, Paul still says the same thing he said on the Damascus road: everything else is rubbish compared to Christ.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

Imagine a man named Fred, about to retire in the year 2001. He spent forty-five years working for one company, and every extra dollar he earned went into that company's stock — his whole retirement, his whole nest egg. He built it into a small fortune. It felt like the wisest decision of his life. The company was Enron. In October of 2001, it collapsed in one of the most infamous bankruptcies in history, and overnight Fred's fortune became zero. The thing he had worked for and trusted in and cherished turned out to be worthless.

That's the kind of reckoning Paul describes in Philippians 3. He too had built a fortune — not money, but religious privilege: Hebrew pedigree, Pharisaic training, blameless law-keeping. By any human measure, his resume was impressive. Then he met Christ on the Damascus road, and he realized it was all loss. In verses 8 through 11, Paul doesn't just repeat that reckoning. He presses further, showing us what true treasure actually is, and why Christ alone is worth everything else combined.

A Decision He Keeps Making

"Indeed, I count everything as loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord."

Philippians 3:8

Look closely at the verb tense. In verse 7, Paul wrote "I counted" — past tense, referring back to his conversion. That's the reckoning every Christian makes at the start: do you see that Christ is worth more than your sin? But in verse 8, Paul shifts to the present tense: "I count." He's writing this some thirty years after Damascus Road, now a prisoner in Rome, and his verdict hasn't changed. After beatings, shipwrecks, and hardship almost beyond counting, he still says the same thing he said on day one. It isn't a passing emotion. It's a settled conviction, renewed daily.

And notice the scope has grown. At his conversion, Paul put his specific religious credentials — the list from verses 5 and 6 — into the loss column. But now he says "everything." Not just his pedigree, but the entire world and everything it offers. This echoes Jesus's own words: what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and lose his soul? John Chrysostom put it beautifully: "When the sun hath appeared, it is lost to sit by a candle." Light a candle outside at noon and it casts no light at all — the sun swallows it whole. That's what the glory of Christ does to every other treasure. Not because those things are evil, but because next to Him, they simply have nothing left to offer.

Losing What Was Already Loss

"For his sake I have suffered the loss of all things and count them as rubbish, in order that I may gain Christ."

Philippians 3:8

There's a play on words here easy to miss if you've read this verse your whole life. Paul says he suffered the loss of things he had already counted as loss. Think about what that actually means: if something is already worthless to you, and then you lose it, have you really lost anything? Imagine a mountain of medical debt you can't pay off. You call to make arrangements, and the person on the phone tells you the hospital lost all record of it — it no longer exists in the system. Is that bad news? Of course not. That's cause to celebrate. A loss of something already counted as loss is, in fact, a gain.

The word Paul uses for "rubbish" is stronger than the English suggests. The King James Version translates it "dung" — it's the Greek word for garbage, sewage, table scraps thrown to the dogs, waste with no use left at all. Paul is saying that everything he once thought valuable — the equivalent today of the best education, a high-paying career, an unimpeachable reputation — is no better than a trash heap compared to gaining Christ. Not because those things have no earthly use whatsoever, but because when it comes to ultimate value, they cannot even enter the competition.

Is a candle helpful for seeing? Absolutely — but not at noon, not when you have the sun.

Notice too what kind of knowing this is. Paul doesn't say "the surpassing worth of knowledge about Christ." He says "Christ Jesus, my Lord" — personal, possessive, relational. This is why 1 Peter 1 says the angels long to look into these things: they behold God's glory directly, but they cannot know Him by faith the way we do. Hebrews 11:1 calls faith "the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen." One day faith will give way to sight. But here and now, we have the strange privilege of trusting what we cannot see, and Paul says that privilege is worth more than anything the eye can see instead.

Found in Him, Not in Yourself

"And be found in him, not having a righteousness of my own that comes from the law, but that which comes through faith in Christ, the righteousness from God that depends on faith."

Philippians 3:9

To know Christ is not only to treasure Him as surpassing worth; it's to be found in Him. This phrase describes union with Christ: to be so joined to Him that His story becomes your story, His death becomes your death, His resurrection becomes your resurrection. Jesus used the same picture: "I am the vine, you are the branches... apart from me you can do nothing." A branch cut off from the vine isn't a branch anymore. It's just a stick. It has no life of its own; it only lives by what flows into it.

Key Point

"And be found in him, not having a righteousness of my own that comes from the law, but that which comes through faith in Christ, the righteousness from God that depends on faith."

How does this union happen? Not through Paul's own righteousness, not through law-keeping or moral effort or heritage or reputation. Paul had tried all of that, and by human standards he'd succeeded at all of it, and still stood condemned before God. Romans 3:20 is blunt: by the works of the law, no human being will be justified in God's sight. Isaiah 64:6 says our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment; no matter how hard we scrub, the stain remains. Righteousness cannot be achieved. It can only be received.

This is the great exchange of the gospel: God made Christ, who knew no sin, to be sin for us, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God. At the cross, Christ takes our filthy garments and covers us instead with His own spotless robe. And Paul repeats the phrase "that depends on faith" almost word for word within the same sentence — not because he's being sloppy, but because our hearts drift back toward self-reliance so easily that the point needs saying twice. The deepest question of your life is not "Am I good enough?" It's "Am I found in Christ?" On the day you stand before God, your resume will not be enough. Only Christ will be enough, and He is enough completely.

Resurrection Power, and Fellowship in Suffering

"That I may know him and the power of his resurrection, and may share his sufferings, becoming like him in his death, that by any means possible I may attain the resurrection from the dead."

Philippians 3:10-11

Paul isn't finished. To know Christ is also to experience the power of His resurrection — not merely to know about it, but to have it at work in you. Romans 6:4 explains: we were buried with Him by baptism into death, so that just as Christ was raised, we too might walk in newness of life. Think about everything Paul endured — imprisonment, beatings, shipwrecks, sleepless nights — and yet he pressed on with joy. Not because he was extraordinary. Because the same power that raised Jesus from the dead was sustaining him day by day. That is the same power available to you today if you are in Christ: power to forgive when your flesh wants revenge, power to persevere when suffering tempts you to quit, power to keep believing when doubt presses in.

But Paul immediately balances resurrection power with something we'd rather skip past: fellowship in Christ's sufferings. Notice the word "share" — the same word used back in chapter 1 for Paul's partnership with the Philippians in the gospel. Suffering for Christ isn't punishment. It's partnership. It's how we're conformed to His likeness, the way gold is refined by fire — suffering strips away pride, loosens our grip on this world, and deepens our dependence on God. Our culture spends billions trying to eliminate discomfort of every kind. Paul says something different: suffering for Christ is not loss, it's gain, because it deepens our fellowship with the One who suffered for us.

None of this expresses doubt about the resurrection to come. It expresses humility and endurance — Paul knows the path to glory runs through suffering, and he's willing to walk it because the destination is certain.

What Are You Counting as Gain?

Two questions rise out of this passage. First: what are you clinging to right now that you think will make you more secure, more valuable? Career success? Financial security? Moral respectability? Even religious activity? Paul makes it clear: all of it is rubbish compared to Christ. None of it can save you. None of it can satisfy your soul.

Second: are you living in the power of His resurrection, or are you quietly trying to live by your own strength again? The Christian life begins by grace and continues by grace. It's not a one-time decision made at conversion and then forgotten. It's a daily reckoning, a repeated decision to count all things as loss because Christ is of surpassing worth. Whatever it costs you this week, remember: in Him, nothing is wasted, nothing is truly lost, and everything that actually matters is gained.
