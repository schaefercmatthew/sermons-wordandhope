Philippians

What Philippians 1:1-2 Packs Into a Greeting

It's easy to read past a letter's opening greeting to get to the "real" content. But Paul packs four load-bearing truths about who you are and how a church is built into just two verses.

By Matthew Schaefer · Pastor, First Baptist Church of Wellston

Philippians is a letter written from one ministry in transition to another. Paul writes it from prison, genuinely uncertain whether he will live or die, to a church he helped plant years earlier in Philippi — the first church Paul ever planted in Europe, born out of a jail cell in Acts 16 when God shook open the doors and saved a jailer's life. It's more than a little fitting that this letter, written from another prison, opens not with Paul's needs but with a reminder to the church of exactly who they are.

That's worth sitting with before rushing past these first two verses as a throwaway greeting. In a church facing genuine uncertainty about its future — not chains, perhaps, but real unknowns all the same — what's needed isn't a clever new strategy. It's a clear gospel identity, and Paul gives us four pieces of it in a single breath: we are slaves of Christ, we are saints in Christ, the church is built to function through two specific offices, and all of it is sustained by grace and peace from God.

Slaves of Christ, Not Under Compulsion but by Love

"Paul and Timothy, servants of Christ Jesus, to all the saints in Christ Jesus who are at Philippi, with the overseers and deacons."

Philippians 1:1

The word translated "servants" here is the Greek doulos, and the most accurate translation is actually "slaves." Not a hired worker, not a volunteer who can walk away when it's inconvenient, but a man who belongs entirely to another. Paul isn't using the word with shame. He's using it with purpose, because it has deep roots in the Old Testament: a Hebrew man drowning in debt could sell himself into slavery, and after six years he was to be released — unless he loved his master, in which case Exodus 21 lets him say plainly, "I love my master; I will not go out free," and stay for life.

That's exactly the logic Paul is pointing to. He's not a slave to Christ under compulsion. He's a slave by love, because he knows what it actually looked like to be in debt to sin, and he knows Christ is a good master. And here's the deeper truth underneath it: before you or I could ever call ourselves bondservants of Christ, Christ had to serve us first. He took on the form of a slave and became obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross, bearing our punishment and purchasing a people with His own blood. You are not your own. You were bought with a price, and the master who bought you is not harsh. His yoke is easy. His burden is light.

Saints Because You're His, Not Because You Earned It

Paul addresses this letter to "all the saints," meaning not just the leadership, but the whole congregation. The word doesn't mean perfect, or morally elite, or dead, or miracle-working. Used 67 times in Scripture, it always means the same simple thing: a Christian, one set apart by God for His purposes. It speaks to your identity, not your conduct.

Notice how Paul locates them: "in Christ Jesus," and also "at Philippi." Every believer lives in two places at once — physically present wherever you actually are, and spiritually rooted in Christ, which is where your real identity, security, and hope come from. Nobody is born a saint. You were born in sin, dead in trespasses, a child of wrath by nature. But God, being rich in mercy, made you alive together with Christ. Sainthood isn't a status you earn by trying harder. It's a standing you receive through union with Christ, who lived the holy life you never could, died to cleanse your guilt, and rose to make you new.

If you belong to Christ, no matter how weak you feel or how much sin you're still fighting, you are a saint. You don't create that identity. You live from it.

How a Church Is Actually Built: Overseers and Deacons

Paul greets the church "with the overseers and deacons" — naming the only two offices the New Testament actually gives to the church. Overseer, elder, shepherd, and (through the Latin) pastor are four different words for the exact same office, each highlighting a different facet of it: overseeing means watching and guarding, eldering means leading, shepherding means feeding the flock. And in every instance except where a single writer refers to himself personally (1 Peter 5, 2 John, 3 John), the word is always plural. The New Testament never shows us a single-pastor-led congregation; that pattern, however common today, is foreign to Scripture. The biblical design is a plurality of qualified, godly men leading together, tested not on personality or preaching style alone but on the character qualifications laid out in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1.

The deacon's role is different but no less vital. The word simply means servant, and the office began in Acts 6 when the apostles, unable to both preach the word and meet the church's practical needs, appointed godly men to do the latter so the word and prayer could stay central. Deacons aren't assistants to the pastor. They're ministers of mercy, carrying real, practical burdens so the church can keep its priorities straight. And like the overseer, a deacon serves not from pride or ambition, but because Christ, who stooped to wash feet and touch lepers and go all the way to the cross, first served him.

Key Point

Paul greets the church "with the overseers and deacons" — naming the only two offices the New Testament actually gives to the church.

Grace and Peace: The Foundation Underneath Everything

Paul closes these two verses with a phrase found, in some form, in all thirteen of his letters: "grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:2). It's not a throwaway line. It's the foundation everything else in this letter stands on. Grace is God's undeserved kindness toward sinners who deserved His judgment — kindness you couldn't earn, and don't have to, because in Christ you are fully accepted, fully forgiven, fully secure. The Christian life doesn't start with grace and then move on to effort. It starts with grace, continues by grace, and ends in grace.

And peace, out of that grace, isn't a feeling that everything is fine. It's an objective truth: you are no longer condemned, no longer God's enemy, no longer a stranger but a child. That doesn't mean trouble disappears — Paul is writing this from a literal prison cell. It means your standing with God is settled regardless of what trouble comes. The war is over. The debt is paid. In Christ, God is not against you; He is for you.

That's the identity a church in transition — or a life in transition — actually needs. Not a clever new strategy, not self-reliant confidence, not fear of what's next, but the plain truth of who you already are: a slave who loves his master, a saint who didn't earn the title, a member of a body built exactly the way Scripture describes, resting entirely on grace and peace that were never yours to produce in the first place.
