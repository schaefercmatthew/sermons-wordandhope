Why Pastors Get Judged Harder Than Everyone Else

Malachi 2 catches God cursing the very priests who were supposed to represent him. The reason he comes down so hard on them is the same reason your pastor is held to a standard you aren't.

Matthew Schaefer · October 4, 2026

If you've ever sat under a pastor who clearly didn't know his Bible, you've felt something was wrong even if you couldn't name it. Maybe he was kind. Maybe he worked hard. But something in you knew that a man standing up to teach God's people ought to actually know what God said, and when he doesn't, it costs more than a bad Sunday morning.

Malachi 2 names that cost directly. God isn't talking here to Israel in general. He's talking to the priests, the ones specifically responsible for teaching the people and representing them before him, and he says their failure is worse than an ordinary failure. The weight of it comes from what they were trusted with, not from how many sins they racked up.

Why the Target Narrows to the Priests

Malachi doesn't open with the priests. Chapter 1 starts with God speaking to Israel as a whole: "I have loved you." Israel's answer is a flat denial dressed up as a question: "How have you loved us?" Then partway through that same chapter, God's attention narrows. He stops addressing the nation in general and zeroes in on one group inside it: "And now, O priests, this command is for you." Out of all of Israel, out of the people God had already set apart for his own purpose, the priests were set apart again, chosen out of the chosen, and given specific instructions for how to represent him.

They didn't keep those instructions. Chapter 1 already showed it: instead of offering the best of the flock the way they'd been commanded, they handed God the worst of it, sick animals, lame animals, blind animals, and called it worship. By chapter 2, God is done describing the failure. He's pronouncing the sentence.

The Text: God's Charge Against the Priests

Here's the passage in full:

And now, O priests, this command is for you. If you will not listen, if you will not take it to heart to give honor to my name, says the LORD of hosts, then I will send the curse upon you and I will curse your blessings. Indeed, I have already cursed them, because you do not lay it to heart. Behold, I will rebuke your offspring, and spread dung on your faces, the dung of your offerings, and you shall be taken away with it. So shall you know that I have sent this command to you, that my covenant with Levi may stand, says the LORD of hosts. My covenant with him was one of life and peace, and I gave them to him. It was a covenant of fear, and he feared me. He stood in awe of my name. True instruction was in his mouth, and no wrong was found on his lips. He walked with me in peace and uprightness, and he turned many from iniquity. For the lips of a priest should guard knowledge, and people should seek instruction from his mouth, for he is the messenger of the LORD of hosts. But you have turned aside from the way. You have caused many to stumble by your instruction. You have corrupted the covenant of Levi, says the LORD of hosts, and so I make you despised and abased before all the people, inasmuch as you do not keep my ways but show partiality in your instruction. Malachi 2:1–9

That's not a warning about a possible future. Twice in the first four verses, God says the curse is already in motion. "I will send the curse" sits right next to "I have already cursed them." Both are true at once. The judgment that's coming is building on judgment that has already landed.

A Diagnosis of a Wound Already There

Think about a kid who keeps touching a hot stove. It hurts every time. He cries every time. And yet he reaches for it again. That's the picture here. The priests were already weeping at the altar, Malachi says a few verses later, because the blessings they expected weren't coming. They could feel that something was wrong. God's answer isn't surprise. It's: I already told you. I've already cursed you once, and if you don't turn back, there's more where that came from. The pain they were already feeling wasn't bad luck. It was the first wave of exactly what God said would happen.

Two Specific Failures, Not One

God names two distinct problems with the priests, and it's worth separating them because they're not the same sin.

The first is corrupt worship, the subject of chapter 1. They weren't honoring God as a great king. They were giving him gifts he never asked for and calling it devotion.

The second shows up starting around verse 8: "You have turned aside from the way. You have caused many to stumble by your instruction." This is a different charge. The priests didn't just worship badly. They taught badly, and in doing so, dragged the rest of Israel down with them. Verse 7 says what a priest was actually for: "The lips of a priest should guard knowledge, and people should seek instruction from his mouth, for he is the messenger of the LORD of hosts." That was the job. Guard the truth. Hand it to the people straight. Instead, verse 9 says they "showed partiality" in their instruction, meaning they bent what they taught to fit whoever was in front of them rather than simply telling the truth.

Why this cuts deeper than ordinary sin

A priest who fails only hurts himself. A priest who misleads takes other people down with him. That's why God's language escalates here. Verse 9 says he will make them "despised and abased before all the people." The judgment matches the scope of the damage, not just the size of the sin.

This should sound familiar if you know the Gospels. Jesus uses almost identical language against the Pharisees centuries later, calling them blind guides leading other blind people into a ditch. And the New Testament is blunt about why teachers face a harder standard: those who teach will be judged with greater strictness. God isn't holding a grudge against teachers. He's accounting for the fact that when a teacher gets it wrong, the damage doesn't stop with him.

What the Curses Actually Describe

Look closely at the specific curses God names, because they're not generic. "I will curse your blessings" targets the present: the food on their table, the breath in their lungs, the ordinary good things they were still receiving even now. "I will rebuke your offspring" reaches into the next generation, the one thing a parent protects hardest. And "spread dung on your faces, the dung of your offerings" is about public disgrace, the most unclean thing possible, smeared across the one part of them everyone would see.

None of this is random. The curse lands exactly where the sin was committed. They dishonored God's name in public worship, so their disgrace comes in public, on their faces, in front of everyone. The punishment doesn't just follow the crime. It mirrors it.

Levi, by Contrast, Shows What Fear Was Supposed to Look Like

In the middle of this judgment, God pauses to describe someone who got it right: Levi, the priestly line's namesake. "My covenant with him was one of life and peace, and I gave them to him. It was a covenant of fear, and he feared me. He stood in awe of my name."

Notice what God does there. He doesn't set fear against life and peace, like fear is the hard part you have to get through before the good part starts. Fear of God and life and peace sit in the same sentence, on the same side, as parts of the same gift. Levi feared God, stood in awe of him, and because of that, he had life and peace. The real contrast in this passage is between Levi, who feared God and received life, and the priests of Malachi's own day, who didn't fear God at all and received dung on their faces instead.

That matters for you even if you're not a pastor. Standing in awe of God, taking him seriously enough that it actually changes how you live, isn't the opposite of peace. It's the road to it. The priests who feared nothing ended up disgraced. The one who feared God walked with him in peace and uprightness.

What This Asks of You Now

If you teach anyone anything about God, a Sunday school class, your own kids at the dinner table, a friend asking a hard question, this passage is for you directly. You don't get to pass along what you assume is true or what sounds right. You're responsible to know what God actually said before you hand it to someone else. If you're not sure, the answer isn't to guess confidently. It's to go find out from someone who knows more than you do before you speak.

If you sit under a pastor, this passage tells you what to actually expect from him. Not charisma. Not comfort. Guarded knowledge, instruction you can trust came from the text and not from what was convenient to say. A congregation has every right to expect that the person teaching them has done the work to know the Bible.

And if you've ever wondered why God seems to come down harder on religious leaders than on anyone else, here's the answer in plain terms: the lips of a priest were supposed to guard knowledge. When they don't, people go looking for truth and get handed something else instead, and they have no way of knowing the difference until the damage is already done.

The priests in Malachi's day couldn't do what they were asked to do. Not partially. Not mostly. They failed at worship and they failed at teaching, and both failures are named in the same breath. That failure is exactly why the rest of Malachi keeps pointing past the temple toward someone better still to come, a priest who would guard the truth perfectly, teach without partiality, and stand in awe of God the way Levi once did. The old priesthood couldn't carry that weight. You were never meant to find your hope in whether your pastor gets it right every time. You were meant to find it in the one priest who already did.
